This page lists some personal advice for getting [research assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_assistant) positions in university[^4] research labs, based on my personal experiences[^3]. This is most helpful for students and people from industry, who might be unfamiliar with university research lab norms.

## Building research lab and mentor relationships

**A few deep relationships.** Identify a few (1-3) labs in areas you're interested in. Focus your efforts on looking at their publications, culture, etc. to see if it fits you, then commit to deep relationships with a few labs. This is unlike job applications in industry, where people send out applications in bulk to 10+ companies.

**Choose a lab based on your interests, rather than lab prestige.** High school and university education often trains students to optimize for the most prestigious schools, circles, and jobs. People in industry tend to choose job opportunities based on this as well. However, this does not apply to higher education and long-term research, where fit matters more than prestige[^1]. If you are considering an academic career, remember that an academic career is a lifelong endeavor, and intrinsic motivators outweigh extrinsic motivators in the long-term.

**Attend lab events, seminars, or after-class office hours.** Engage with the research *ideas* first, as a test if those areas interest you. Do not engage solely with the intent of getting in. You are testing your interest. Labs tend to hire people they already know (i.e. current students associated with the lab, or students of the PI/[principal investigator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_investigator)), and being present acquaints yourself with the community. If we imagine a lab community as a graph, with the innermost nodes most involved in the work and community of the lab, all nodes begin at the outermost edges. Outermost nodes start by taking low-risk commitments that familiarize them with the network (i.e. being present, then doing low-stakes tasks, then committing to positions, etc.), and as they take on more central work, these nodes start to move towards the center[^2].

## Knowing the research grant system and publication targets

**Actively ask for open positions.** For many labs, RA positions are often not advertised or announced on the lab's site. Sometimes, PIs may post to social media that they are hiring, although this varies. It's best to just ask the PI if there are any open positions[^6]. If none are open at the time, ask when a slot might open up.

**Unpaid roles may still be available even when no slots are listed.** RA roles may be paid or unpaid. Slots usually refer to paid positions, hence PIs may still take in unpaid RAs. It's generally socially acceptable to ask PIs if you can collaborate as an unpaid RA.

**Plan around grant cycles.** In industry, job applications usually peak around June-July, or near the end of a [fiscal year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiscal_year). In university research, the equivalent for this is research grant cycles. Each research project needs [funding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funding_of_science). Usually, labs apply for research grants yearly to get project funding, file the necessary paperwork (ethics clearance, etc.), wait for approval, and projects only start once approved[^5]. Filing and approval take months, and the exact months vary by university and funding source, so ask your PI about the timeline for research grant cycles so you can ask then if new RA slots have opened up, and so you can plan around when to make time for RA commitments.

**Publication targets.** When PIs file for project approval, they are usually required to propose a project timeline as well. This project timeline will have publication targets. For example, the lab may be required to publish two papers in Scopus-indexed journals by November 2026 for Project X. Depending on the funding source, there are various consequences for failing these publication targets. For example, if publication targets fail to be met, the funding source may block the lab from applying for future grants. If you get a role, or are considering an open role, consider asking for the publication targets so you can decide whether to commit. While student researchers can often get away with cramming their thesis, this is more difficult as an RA given publication targets, since you need to account for how long before the conference/journal accepts your paper, and what to do in case the paper is rejected so that the lab can still meet the publication targets.

[^1]: This is generally true of any higher education pursuits in general, including choosing where to take your masters or PhD. Your lab and mentors shape what work you end up doing, which often leads you to continue doing more of that work.

[^2]: [Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation](https://www.cambridge.org/highereducation/books/situated-learning/6915ABD21C8E4619F750A4D4ACA616CD) develops this idea and shows example across different social networks/communities.

[^3]: My own experiences are based on norms in university research labs in the Philippines, and other cultures may differ.

[^4]: Norms in industry research labs differ, and this advice might not apply.

[^5]: This is especially true for areas of study that need equipment, large-scale compute, and other materials and supplies. For fields that don't necessarily need this (i.e. mathematics or computer science), most of project funding goes to publication fees, salaries, etc.

[^6]: If a lab declared in their project proposal to a funding source that they will ask for funding for an RA position, there is sometimes pressure on the lab to actually spend it. Some funding sources may hold it against the research lab if the funding they declared for RA positions doesn't end up being used (i.e. this may happen if the lab doesn't find a suitable candidate for an RA position). However, I'm not sure how much this varies by funding source.
