---
title: "Why do we define ourselves by what we do, rather than what we think?"
date: 2026-11-02
updated: 2026-11-02
tags: ["culture"]
---

We tend to mainly trace the outward events in our lives, sometimes the ideas and beliefs we are known for (in their final and polished form), and only rarely the evolution of ideas in one's life. It's as though we treat the evolution of ideas as a byproduct of the polished idea, rather than the polished idea being the byproduct of that joyful struggle one spends their life on, and which defines one's life.

## On introductions

An introduction on social media is typically "CEO@$X$" or "Undergrad@$Y$." In conversation, the common opener "tell me about yourself" is often answered by "I'm an $X$ at $Y$." Getting to know another starts with the greeting "what do you do?" and never "what do you think?" or "what do you believe in?" Sharing what you *do* is regarded as easy, low-effort, and socially safe. Sharing what you *think* or *believe* in is regarded as vulnerable, high-effort, and reserved for those whom we have built social depth with.

Yet even then, majority of our social acquaintances, friends, and coworkers, are people we constantly *do* things with: co-work, go out, eat, exercise, drink, etc. Despite knowing them for months and even years, very often we do not know each other's thoughts or beliefs. One might even say they don't know another's "deeper" thoughts or beliefs. Yet what makes one's thoughts or beliefs inherently any "deeper" than one's actions?

## On social media

Majority of social media stories and updates are of *events*: announcements or personal experiences, often involving others, in the form of pictures, videos, or text. Relatively little of social media updates are of *ideas*. It's as if the default is to think one's day-to-day are the external activities in their life, far more often than the internal activities: daily thoughts, emotions, and ideas. It seems uncommon to intentionally observe or articulate one's inner life, even to oneself. Even rarer still is the act of sharing it.

## On biographies

Why is it that biographies are stories of what a person has *done* or what has been *done* to them throughout their life (that is, born in $X$, studied at $Y$, died of $Z$, etc.), and rarely ever the evolution of ideas and beliefs throughout their lives? When Einstein was asked to pen his autobiography, he wrote relatively little of what he *did*. Instead, he wrote about the evolution of his ideas that led to his breakthroughs in general and special relativity, among others. Even when biographies do mention ideas, often it is in the form of "$A$ published $XYZ$, which discusses..." This is still more about the *action* of publishing ideas rather than the conception and description of the idea itself.

In studying the history of a field, it traces the thinkers and events, and by consequence the evolution of ideas. Yet this is more uncommon in both the study of the history of a person, and even more uncommon in how people retell their own lives. Ask anyone to tell you about their life, and most can easily recount the history of events, but not the history of their ideas and beliefs.
