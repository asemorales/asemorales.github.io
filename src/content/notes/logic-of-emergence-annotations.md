## Abstract

[“Emergence”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergence) refers to the fact that different mathematical languages and theories seem to be required to describe scientific theories that operate at different spatial and temporal scales. Highly circumscribed, special cases of emergence have been investigated using many different formal tools, including [coarse-graining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coarse-grained_modeling) (over space and / or time), [renormalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renormalization), [dimension reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionality_reduction), [broken symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_breaking), and various formulations of [causality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_model). Here we extend the mathematics of [model theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_theory) to unify and extend these earlier investigations. As a preliminary application of our framework, we investigate canonical examples of emergence, including the emergence of genetics from chemistry, of psychology from neurobiology, and of different forms of [collective behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_behavior) from the laws governing their constituent parts. By exploiting the power of model theory we also uncover many aspects of emergence that were previously obscured. In particular, we uncover some of the rich hierarchical structure that relates different scientific fields. For example, we show that ==scientific fields can be related via a collection of multiple partial orders, all defined over the same set==. On the other hand, ==under an alternative relation among scientific fields, one which allows cycles, we show that it is possible that the set of all of scientific fields must collapse into a single scientific field==. Going forward, these investigations point to the formal tools that might need to be developed in order to arrive at a deeper understanding of this emergent hierarchy of scientific reality.

## The Problem with Emergence

### What’s been done before

Anderson’s “More is Different”[^cite-wk-2] remains the foundational text for many researchers seeking to investigate the concept of emergence. Anderson’s core argument is that the laws governing a system at one scale do not determine the organizational principles at a larger scale, because the larger-scale description involves broken symmetries that are not deducible from the underlying equations. A crystal obeys [quantum electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_electrodynamics), but QED does not predict the crystal structure, because the [ground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state) breaks the [rotational symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_symmetry) of the [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_(quantum_mechanics))[^1]. Anderson’s account is physical, dynamical, and tied to specific mechanisms ([spontaneous symmetry breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneous_symmetry_breaking), the [renormalization group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renormalization_group)). It has been productive within [condensed matter physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics) and chemistry but struggles as a general theory of emergence across the sciences whose relationships are extremely complicated, approximate, and probabilistic.

Taking a more philosophical rather than mathematical approach, Chalmers[^cite-wk-12] introduced a distinction between weak and strong emergence. [[glossary#weak emergence (noun)|Weak emergence]] obtains when the higher-level phenomenon is unexpected given the base-level laws but is in principle deducible from them. [[glossary#strong emergence (noun)|Strong emergence]] obtains when the higher-level phenomenon is not deducible even in principle, and new, “transphysical” laws are required. [Consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hard_problem_of_consciousness) was Chalmer’s preferred candidate for strong emergence, where the physical facts do not fix the phenomenal or psychological facts[^3]. Strong emergence presupposes a notion of deducibility that is rarely made precise, and it tends to mix [ontology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontology) (what exists), [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology) (what can be known), and experimental methods (what can be measured). The idea of [property dualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Property_dualism) (brain and mind are incommensurate) that underlies strong emergence[^cite-wk-11] is either a complicated variety of Anderson’s scaling argument, or a stronger claim for [epistemological irreducibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explanatory_gap).

The idea of [multiple realizability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_realizability) is the observation that the same higher-level property of a so-called “[special science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_sciences)” (so properties like reproductive fitness, pain, warfare) can be realized by different lower-level substrates[^8][^cite-wk-31][^cite-wk-15]. It has become a general argument for the autonomy[^6] of those special sciences, that they can meaningfully be considered without consideration of the lower-level substrates[^7]. This can be phrased in Chalmerian terms as saying that the special sciences are weakly emergent from the lower-level ones, strictly speaking, but they can be considered to be effectively strongly emergent.

This is closely related to the question “what is a [macrostate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microstate_(statistical_mechanics)#:~:text=a%20macrostate%20of%20a%20system,given%20analysis)?” and has been taken up by several authors seeking to make coarse-graining precise[^cite-wk-13][^cite-wk-5][^cite-wk-19][^cite-wk-14]. One approach to this issue grounded in physics partitions a microscopic state space into bins[^5], declares each bin a macrostate, and studies the induced dynamics on bins. Many of these studies have emphasized a commutativity requirement wherein [...] coarse-graining is adequate when coarse-graining the dynamics gives the same result as first evolving and then coarse-graining[^cite-wk-43][^cite-wk-30][^cite-wk-9][^cite-wk-10]. However, many features of various scientific fields have nothing to do with dynamics in the first place. This is especially true of the special sciences.

Beyond coarse-graining, aspects of emergence have been approached from the perspective of [criticality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_phenomena) and [phase transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_transition) (and therefore renormalization group theory), the behavior of systems in specific limits, particularly of large numbers, and algorithmic or statistical complexity. Jensen’s work[^cite-wk-20] on [self-organized criticality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-organized_criticality) proposed that many complex systems spontaneously tune themselves to a critical point, producing [scale-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance) behavior (power laws, long-range correlations) without the fine-tuning of an external control parameter[^10]. However, again, it is hard to see how this captures the rich and complicated relationships among the special sciences and their lower-level substrates. Reproductive fitness, pain and computation are far more than simply being phase transitions or critical points of some sort.

Building on work stretching back to Nagel[^cite-wk-27], Palacios[^cite-wk-29] has explored the relationship between approximation and idealization in [inter-theory reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertheoretic_reduction), arguing that the philosophical literature on emergence has underestimated the role of limits ([thermodynamic limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_limit), [continuum limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_limit)) in connecting levels. ==Ay and collaborators[^cite-wk-4][^cite-wk-3] propose that emergence is measured by the degree to which the [joint probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_probability_distribution) of the system’s components cannot be factored into a product of independent marginals, and so the system is more than the sum of its parts in a precise [information-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_theory) sense.==

### What is missing in all earlier frameworks on emergence

It is not immediately obvious how to go about connecting or integrating these several perspectives. Anderson’s account describes symmetry and invariance, Chalmers’s deducibility. Multiple realizability is about the many-to-one character of the micro-macro map, whereas the macrostate literature captures the adequacy of coarse-grainings. Jensen’s is concerned with the parsimonious origin of new structures, whereas Ay and Polani seek to quantify the informational irreducibility of wholes to parts.

Furthermore, there are several core features of emergence that are not touched on in any of those frameworks. Most obviously, the different scientific disciplines have different vocabularies, i.e., the constituent elements of one discipline will in general contain concepts that are wholly absent from another discipline. The foundational tools and concepts of different scientific disciplines can by definition appear [incommensurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commensurability_(philosophy_of_science)).

As an example, [classical Newtonian dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_mechanics) is all about positions, velocities, and forces, which govern the time courses of systems specified with initial positions and velocities. In contrast, [Mendelian genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendelian_inheritance) is all about genes, alleles, (co-)dominance, recessive traits, phenotypes, etc. Nothing to do with time, nothing to do with velocities or forces. Nor does classical dynamics have anything to do with phenotypes or alleles. In no sense is one discipline recovered from the other after coarse-graining some state space. They are even concerned with wholly different kinds of phenomena. Newtonian dynamics is all about, well, dynamics, and about systems moving through an infinite sequence of states as time evolves. Yet equilibrium theories of genetics, such as the [Hardy Weinberg distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy%E2%80%93Weinberg_principle), don’t involve detailed trajectories through state space.

Despite these differences, there are features that both Newtonian mechanics and Mendelian genetics do have in common. Crucially, both adhere to the strictures of [(first order) logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_logic). The [logical AND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_conjunction) of two propositions is perfectly well-defined in both of them, for example. Evidently, classical dynamics and Mendelian dynamics are magisteria that overlap – but only partially. How then can we relate them?

These earlier approaches share a limitation that the framework developed below is designed to either overcome, or at the very least, expose their limitations and highlight what a future research program on emergence should take seriously. A significant limitation that is immediately apparent is that ==each presupposes a single shared state space that is merely partitioned, coarse-grained, or tuned to a critical point.== They therefore cannot even state three features that are central to the relations among the special sciences[^11]. The first is the ==use of genuinely different vocabularies, and the signature of the emergent discipline need not be a coarsening of the signature of the lower one==, as it is not for genetics relative to chemistry. The second is the ==existence of non-dynamical sciences==, such as Mendelian genetics, whose content is not a statement about trajectories but equilibrium configurations. The third is the ==probabilistic and partial character of many inter-level relations, which requires the convergence of multiple overlapping space and time scales.== We need to move beyond a shared microscopic state space.

### Framing Emergence

Some work towards such a macroscopic view of emergence has been attempted earlier, using aspects of model theory[^cite-wk-24][^cite-wk-17] and other tools of mathematical logic, specifically first-order logic. Nagel’s *The Structure of Science*[^cite-wk-27] proposed that inter-theory reduction (a weak form of the emergence relationship between phenomena and theories) consists in deriving the sentences of one theory from those of another, supplemented by “bridge laws” connecting the vocabularies. Nagel treats reduction as a logical relation between sets of sentences, not as a relation between materials. The framework is perhaps too restrictive and fails to account for approximation, idealization, and the role of limiting procedures. Tarski’s related work on truth and semantic concepts[^cite-wk-35][^cite-wk-36] established that the meaning of a formal theory is given by its models. This approach to scientific theories, developed further by Suppes[^cite-wk-32][^cite-wk-33] and van Fraassen[^cite-wk-39], treats a scientific theory not as Nagel does, as a set of sentences in a formal language (the “syntactic” view) but as a class of structures (the “semantic” view). Bunge’s systematic ontology[^cite-wk-6][^cite-wk-7] attempted a comprehensive formal framework for levels of organization, emergence, and the hierarchy of the sciences, using the language of set theory and lattice theory. Bunge insisted that emergence is an ontological category, not merely an epistemological one, and that it requires a formal account of what it means for properties at one level to be “novel” relative to properties at another.

The framework we develop here takes up the natural scientific theories of emergence and situate them in logical program of Nagel, the semantics in Tarski and Suppes, while respecting the ontological emphasis in Bunge. The central concept is interpretability which describes the construction of one formal vocabulary inside another. This concept is general enough to capture certain aspects of coarse-graining and multiple realizability, as well as Nagelian reduction. Here we extend this framework to include new concepts like probabilistic emergence, stochastic emergence, approximate emergence, multiple emergence (in which one discipline emerges from the combination of multiple other disciplines, not just one), and observer-relative emergence. These extensions allow us to formally explore the full breadth of the idea of emergence, in in all of its richness. And the framework surfaces many aspects of emergence that have not even been appreciated before, never mind investigated formally.

Central to our approach is connecting many features of emergent phenomena with associated formal concepts from model theory. These include:

- A specific **physical system**, which is represented in our framework as a model-theoretic “structure”. An example is the structure of $N$ point masses orbiting one another under Newtonian gravity. Another example is a set of $M$ moving electrons in vacuum that are close to one another.
- A set of “laws”, or a **scientific formalism**. In our framework, a formalism is represented as a “first-order logic theory”. Any physical system that is governed by a particular formalism is represented in our framework as an associated structure that satisfies the theory of that formalism. An example of a scientific formalism is Newton’s laws, including the precise functions, relations and constants of those laws. Another example is classical [Hamiltonian dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_mechanics), which involves different functions, relations and constants from Newton’s laws.
- A **scientific discipline** comprises the collection of all scientific formalisms that are reformulations of one another, a property that we identify in model-theoretic terms as those first-order theories being “mutually interpretable”. An example is the collection of Newton’s laws, Lagrangian dynamics, Hamiltonian dynamics, etc., all of which are formalisms in the scientific discipline of classical dynamics.

  Any structure that satisfies one theory $T$ also satisfies any other theory $T^{\prime}$ mutually interpretable with $T$. (This is a simple consequence of the fact that “mutual interpretability’ is a transitive relation, as described below.) In our approach, this is reflected in the fact that any physical system that is governed by one scientific formalism in a scientific discipline is also governed by all the other formalisms in that discipline. So in particular, any of those other formalisms can be used to describe that physical system.
- And finally, the emergence of one scientific discipline from another, which is formalized in our framework using extensions of the model-theoretic concept of non-mutual interpretability. An example is the emergence of classical dynamics from quantum dynamics.

While these maps from aspects of emergence in the sciences to concepts in model theory lays a foundation for formalizing emergence, they leave out many other aspects of emergence that are central to the scientific enterprise. To capture all the nuances of emergence in the sciences, we need to extend basic model theoretic constructions in several ways.

One particularly important extension of model theory we introduce is designed to capture propagation of a probability measure from the state space of a microstate-level description of a system to a probability measure over the state space of a macrostate-level description of the same system. Loosely speaking, in the language of model theory this is a way to propagate a measure from the domain of discourse of a structure satisfying a theory $B$ to the domain of discourse of a structure satisfying a theory $A$ that is interpreted in $B$. This extension is needed for example to formalize how a thermodynamic description of a physical system emerges from a statistical physics description of the same system.

A closely related extension of model theory we also need to introduce is stochastic emergence. In this case a probability measure across a set of physical systems described by a scientific formalism $B$ leads to a probability distribution over the *laws* in a scientific formalism $A$ that emerges from $B$. This extension is needed for example to formalize how a probability distribution over the joint states of the neurons in a brain leads to a probability distribution over the behavioral laws of the organism housing that brain.

Yet another important extension of model theory we need to introduce arises from the fact that often one scientific formalism emerges from *multiple* other formalisms, not just one. As a simple example, biomechanics emerges from the combination of biochemistry and Newtonian mechanics, together with molecular biology. And perhaps less obviously, when an existing feature or trait experiences a change in context, giving rise to an apparently new function: what evolutionary biologists call an exaptation. Finally, we also introduce an extension to allow for *approximate* emergence, in which one scientific discipline doesn’t precisely arise from another one – but does to within some error tolerance.

Because our framework is built from model theory and the associated concept of interpretation, formulation of emergence applies to familiar examples in the mathematics literature of model-theoretic interpretation, e,g., in our framework rational numbers “emerge” from the integers. Our framework also captures a very narrowly focused notion of “emergence” commonly found in the physics literature, as a commutativity property relating the dynamics of systems to the dynamics of coarse-grainings of those systems. Going further, our framework captures how high-level computer programs “emerge” from low-level compiled versions of those programs verified through denotational semantics, as well as how the ideal gas law is described as emerging from Newtonian mechanics. The concept of “broken symmetry” from condensed matter physics, invoked in the canonical paper by Anderson, is another special case of emergence captured in our framework.

## Model Theory

Every equation in every textbook or research paper involves functions, relations, and / or constants. Moreover, either explicitly or implicitly, they all involve the Boolean operators (logical NOT, AND, etc.), the existential and universal quantifiers, and of course the equals symbol. The minimal mathematics that involves all such equations is called “first-order logic”, or “predicate logic”. Particular instances of such equations — specific examples of physical systems that satisfy such equations — are called “structures”. Accordingly, the minimal mathematics that captures all equations in science is first order logic, and the minimal mathematics that captures all examples of those equations is the theory of structures. The combination of first order logic and the theory of structures is known as model theory (a term coined by Tarski).

In this section we present a very cursory overview of those elements of model theory that we shall exploit in this paper. The reader is directed to the literature for more details, e.g.,[^cite-wk-24][^cite-wk-17].

### First order languages

While terminology differs throughout the literature, in first-order logic we usually use the term **signature** to mean a collection of three different sets of **extra-logical symbols**, namely a set of function symbols (each with a specified arity), a set of relation symbols (each with a specified arity), and a set of constant symbols. (As an aside, we note that constants are simply zero-arity functions, and an $n$-ary function is a special $(n{+}1)$-ary relation.) The extra-logical symbols in a signature carry no intrinsic meaning, and as described below, they are names awaiting interpretation (they are “signifiers”, in effect). As an example, a signature used to analyze arithmetic might include the symbols $\{0,1,+,\times\}$.

As mentioned above, every equation in every scientific paper involves some set of extra-logic symbols, signifying functions, relations and constants. In addition to a set of extra-logical symbols, which might vary from one scientific paper to another, all equations in every scientific paper also involve the same equality symbol. Moreover, whenever such equations are combined, the standard Boolean logic symbols come into play (even if only implicitly). To reflect this, we define the **logical symbols** of first-order logic as the Boolean functions ($\land,\lor,\lnot$),[^authors-1]the quantifiers ($\forall,\exists$), and the equality symbol ($=$). It is also always assumed that the set of extra-logical symbols contains an infinite set of variables, typically written as lower-case latin characters. Conventionally, an infinite set of left and right parentheses are also included in the set of logical symbols, not because they are necessary for the formalism, but to help with readability. Similarly, we will use the symbol ‘:’ to demarcate existential quantifiers at the beginning of a formula from the rest of the formula.

A **language** $\mathcal{L}$ is an infinite set of strings, given by the closure of the operation of concatenating the symbols in a signature and the logical symbols according to the logical apparatus of first-order logic. In other words, it is the closure under the recursive rules of an appropriate grammar for building strings of logical and extra-logical symbols that form **(well-formed) formulas**.

As an example, $\exists\exists=\lnot x$ is not a well-formed formula, and so not a string in any language. On the other hand, in the signature $\{0,1,+,\times\}$, $\forall x\exists y(x+y=0)$ is a well-formed formula, as is $\forall x\exists y(x+y=y+x)$. While different languages have different sets of extra-logical symbols, they all share the same set of logical symbols.

If a variable in a formula is quantified (with either $\forall$ or $\exists$), it a **bound** variable. If a variable is not bound, then it is **free**. If a formula has any free variables then it is **open**. If instead all the variables in a formula are bound, the formula is closed, and sometimes called a **sentence**. As an example, in the signature $\{0,1,+\}$, $x+y=0$ is an open formula, while $\forall x\exists y(x+y=y+x)$ is a sentence.

Here we do not consider second-order model theory, based on second-order logic. While that ultimately might prove necessary – and should be relatively straightforward – most of the work on model theory contents itself with first order logic. Moreover, first order logic suffices for our purposes here.

### Structures

A structure for a signature $\Sigma$ is where the functions, relations and constant of $\Sigma$ are realized as operations on a set. Formally, a **structure** $\mathcal{A}$ is a **domain** $A$ (a non-empty set) together with actual functions $f^{\mathcal{A}}:A^{n}\to A$ for each $n$-ary function symbol, actual relations $R^{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq A^{n}$ for each $n$-ary relation symbol, and actual elements $c^{\mathcal{A}}\in A$ for each constant symbol. It is common to use the term **interpretation** to refer to the map from each extra-logical symbol in a language to the associated entity in the domain of a structure. (Unfortunately, this conflicts with the meaning of “interpretation” between structures, introduced below. The context should always make the meaning clear.)

As an example of these definitions, the signature $\{+,\times,0,1\}$, consisting of two binary functions and two constants, is shared by the counting numbers, the integers, the rationals, and any commutative ring. There are several ways to distinguish those four mathematical systems, despite the fact that they have the same signature. One way is to interpret each of them into a structure. This requires specifying domains for each of them, and identifying the extra-logical symbols in each of them with specific functions, relations and constants in those domains. To illustrate, in all four of those systems the domain is a countable set, and for any element $x$ in the domain, $x+0=x$. On the other hand, for the case of the integers, for any $x$ in the domain there exists some $y$ such that $x+y=0$, but this is not true for the case of the counting numbers.

Note that we can interpret each of those four systems into an infinite set of structures. For example, let $A$ be an arbitrary countably bi-infinite set, with elements $\{\ldots,-1,0,1,\ldots\}$. That set can serve as the domain of the integers, where the symbol $+$ is interpreted as addition in the usual way. Alternatively, let $Y$ be the countably bi-infinite set with elements $\{\ldots,e^{-1},e^{0},e^{1},\ldots\}$. This set as well could serve as the domain of the integers, so long as we now interpret $+$ as multiplication in the usual way. This provides just two of the infinite set of structures one can use to interpret the language of the integers.

An important distinction to bear in mind is that between a structure and its domain. For example, we can define a structure of the real numbers, using signature $\{0,1,\times,+,(\cdot)^{-1},-(\cdot)\}$, and domain ${\mathbb{R}}$. That domain is distinct from the full structure; it is just a set, without any associated properties.

An important aspect of scientific theories is that they typically involve multiple kinds of variable, e.g., positions, temperatures, pressures, categorical states (e.g., sleeping vs. waking vs. exercising), genotype frequencies and fitness values. A convenient way to capture this is with a **many-sorted** structure and an associated language. In such a structure the domain is partitioned into different subsets, each identified with a distinct **sort**. The maps over the domain corresponding to the extra-logical symbols (i.e., the functions, relations and constants) are modified so that each of their arguments specify which sort it comes from. Similarly, the extra-logical symbols in a many-sorted language specify the sorts of the associated arguments. In addition, the infinite set of variables of the language are replaced by a collection of infinite sets of variables, one set for each sort. (Many-sorted structures can be reduced to single-sorted ones, by introducing unary predicates as sort labels, but doing this obscures the content.)

To recapitulate, every *equation* in any research paper or textbook involves languages, either explicitly or implicitly. In other words, it is a WFF. On the other hand, the *examples* presented in every such research paper or textbook has a domain over which those functions, relations and / or constants are defined, either explicitly or implicitly.

However, all those research papers and / or textbooks need a way to reason about the functions, relations and constants, and associated formulas. They need a way to be able combine those functions, etc., and investigate the consequences of those combinations. More generally, they need a way to specify a set of laws that apply to all such examples / structures. In the common vernacular,, they need a “scientific theory”. This is where logic comes in, and it provides the stitching needles and the glue for reaching conclusions about structures and theories. These needles and glue are the topic of the next subsection.

### Theories and Models

Consider a formula $\phi(\bar{a})$ in a language ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$, where $\bar{a}=(a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{m}})$ is shorthand for a set of $m\geq 0$ free variables. Let ${\mathcal{A}}$ be a structure, with functions, relations and constants defined for every extra-logical symbol in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$. In general, if we adopt the standard interpretation of the Boolean logical symbols, then $\phi(\bar{a})$ may or may not hold in ${\mathcal{A}}$ for some specific tuple $\bar{a}$. If it does, we write

$$
\begin{equation}
{\mathcal{A}}\models\phi(\bar{a}), \label{eq:1}
\end{equation}
$$

and say that $\phi(\bar{a})$ is **true** in structure ${\mathcal{A}}$. Alternatively, we sometimes say that ${\mathcal{A}}$ **satisfies** or **models** $\phi(\bar{a})$. If $\phi$ is a sentence, we simply write “${\mathcal{A}}\models\phi$”.

Given a set of sentences $\{\varphi_{i}\}$, we say that a structure satisfies that set, or is a model of that set, if it satisfies each of those sentences individually. We say that an additional sentence $\xi$ is a **(logical) consequence** of $\{\varphi_{i}\}$ if every structure that simultaneously satisfies every sentence in $\{\varphi_{i}\}$ also satisfies $\xi$. Equivalently, we say that $\{\varphi_{i}\}$ **proves** $\xi$, and write $\{\varphi_{i}\}\models\xi$.

The definition of logical consequence can be quite awkward to work with, since one needs to consider all structures with given properties. As a shortcut, a **deductive system** is a purely syntactic set of rules for generating all logical consequences of a given set of sentences. We use the turnstile symbol, $\vdash$, to indicate that one formula (or set of formulas) implies another under some deductive system. As an example, suppose that the symbol $\rightarrow$ means material implication, as in propositional logic. Then if we have both the sentence $\varphi$ and the sentence $\varphi\rightarrow\xi$, the “natural deductive system” would generate the sentence $\xi$. There are many different deductive systems, and we won’t need to pick out one in this paper.

An ${\mathcal{L}}$-$\mathbf{theory}$ $T$ is a set of sentences in the language ${\mathcal{L}}$. (Note that simply by using the logical AND, any finite set of finite sentences can be combined into a single, finite sentence.) Often theories of interest will be those for which a set of sentences can serve as a sensible set of **axioms**, from which other sentences can be derived. As an example, in a language with the binary function symbol $+$ and the constant symbol $0$, a possible sentence to include in an associated theory is $\forall x(x+0=x)$. Sometimes in the literature however, the term “theory” instead means the set of all logical consequences of the axioms. As always, the context should make the meaning clear.

As an example, the sentence $\forall x\exists y:x+y=0$ can be used as an axiom of the theory of the integers. However, it is not one of the axioms of the counting numbers – and in fact is not satisfied in any structure satisfying the axioms of the counting numbers. This illustrates that ultimately, it is not the (shared) signature $\{+,\times,0,1\}$ that distinguishes mathematical systems, but the associated axioms and resultant theories that are expressed in that signature, the resultant truths expressible in it and the domains over which those truths hold.

A theory is not necessarily associated with any particular structure (rather, simply with a language), but it will become “semantically meaningful” when interpreted in that context. We say that a structure is a **model** of a theory $T$, or that $\mathcal{A}$ **satisfies** $T$, and write $\mathcal{A}\models T$, if every sentence in $T$ holds in $\mathcal{A}$.

**Example 2.1**. The language of group theory is the tuple $\{0,\times,\cdot^{-1}\}$, where $\times$ is a binary function and $\cdot^{-1}$ is a unary function. The axioms are ^ex-2-1

1. $\forall x:x\times 0=x$;
2. $\forall x:x\times x^{-1}=0$;
3. $\forall x,y,z:(x\times y)\times z=x\times(y\times z)$;

The language of Abelian group theory is identical, and the axioms are also the same, with the addition of a commutativity axiom:

<!-- markdownlint-disable-next-line MD029 -->
4. $\forall x,y:x\times y=y\times x$;

To illustrate, addition over the integers mod $10$ is a model of the set of all four axioms of commutative group theory (if we interpret the binary operation of the group as addition mod $10$). On the other hand, the set of $\pi/2$ rotations about any of the three orthogonal axes of $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ are a model of the group axioms, but not of the full set of axioms of commutative groups. A logical consequence of the axioms of group theory is, $\forall x,\exists y:y\times(x\times x)=0$. A logical consequence of the axioms of commutative group theory, which does not hold for all groups, is $\forall x,\forall y:y\times(x\times x)=(x\times x)\times y$.

The class of all models of a theory $T$ is written $\mathrm{Mod}(T)$, and is called an **elementary class**. Conversely, the **complete** theory of a structure, $\mathrm{Th}(\mathcal{A})$, is the set of all first-order sentences true in $\mathcal{A}$. Two structures are **elementarily equivalent** if they have the same complete theory. We say that a set of sentences is **consistent** if they have at least one model.[^authors-2]

A theory $T$ is **semantically complete** for a particular deductive system, if for all sentences $\varphi$, $T\models\varphi$ implies $T\vdash\varphi$. $T$ is **sound** for that deductive systems if the converse holds: $T\vdash\varphi$ implies $T\models\varphi$. In contrast, a theory is **(syntactically) complete** if for every sentence $\varphi$ in its language, either $T\models\varphi$ or $T\models\lnot\varphi$. Stated differently, all models of $T$ agree with one another about the truth of all sentences. If a theory is syntactically complete then it is consistent. From now on, unless explicitly stated otherwise, whenever we refer to a theory as being “complete” we mean it is syntactically complete.

Most scientific theories are incomplete. For example, Hamiltonian mechanics admits pairs of physical systems that both satisfy Hamilton’s equations, but disagree on other sentences besides those that Hamilton wrote. (As an illustration, the particles in one system may be gravitationally bound, while some of the particles in the other system eventually escape to infinity.) In general, if we don’t explicitly say that a given theory is complete, then it should be assumed that the theory is a set of axioms from which other sentences can be deduced. Syntactic incompleteness is also rife in mathematics. For example, Euclid’s axioms are incomplete, neither establishing the truth or the falsehood of the parallel line hypothesis.

Let $T$ be any theory that includes the axioms of Peano arithmetic, and $\varphi$ any sentence that is a logical consequence of those axioms. Gödel’s **completeness** theorem for first-order logic guarantees that for the common deductive systems, $T$ is semantically complete. However, neither semantic completeness nor soundness means that every sentence $\varphi$ is a logical consequence of $T$, i.e., they do not imply syntactic completeness. Indeed, define a set of sentences $S$ to be “effectively enumerable” if there is an associated Turing machine that produces a list of sentences as it runs, and every one of the sentences in $S$ will eventually appear in that list. Loosely speaking, Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem says that $T$ cannot be both consistent and syntactically complete if it includes Peano arithmetic, and if its axioms are effectively enumerable.[^authors-3]

**Isomorphism** between structures is defined in the natural way, as a bijection between their domains and their signatures that preserves the interpretations into the domains of the functions, relations and constants. Recall that elementarily equivalent structures are those that satisfy exactly the same sentences. In general, if two structures are isomorphic, they are elementarily equivalent. Moreover, the converse holds if the domain of the structure is finite. However, it does not hold in general if the domain of the structure is infinite, due to the Lowenheim-Skolem theorem. So for example, there are structures that both satisfy the axioms of Peano arithmetic, but are not isomorphic. This means there are some structures that model Peano arithmetic but that differ from the standard structure ${\mathbb{Z}}$ we all learn in grade school. These “non-standard” structures of arithmetic are very counter-intuitive[^cite-wk-17][^cite-wk-24].

For any structure $\mathcal{A}$ with domain $A$, a set $S\subseteq A^{n}$ is **definable** if there is an open formula $\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})$ in the language of $\mathcal{A}$ such that $S=\{(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}):\mathcal{A}\models\varphi[b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]\}$. This has exactly the intuitive meaning for the term “definable” one would want: a subset is definable if we can *define* it mathematically using the language $\mathcal{A}$.

To illustrate this concept, return to the axioms of groups, discussed in [[#^ex-2-1|Example 2.1]]. Suppose we extend the language considered there to include constants $h_{1},h_{2}$. Then in any model of a group, the left coset of $H:=\{h_{1},h_{2}\}$ provides a collection of one-dimensional definable sets, $\{(x\times h_{1})\vee(x\times h_{2})\}$. Similarly, $(x\times y=0)\vee(x\times y\times y)=0$ is a two-dimensional definable set. If the group is Abelian, that two-dimensional definable set is also an equivalence relation.

Note that the $n$ elements of $S$ in the definition of a definable set are ordered. This allows us to apply the same construction to talk about definable relations, and so definable functions. (Functions are definable when the graphs specified by their relations are definable sets.) Note as well that whether a set is definable in a structure depends not only on the domain of that structure, but also on the precise signature of that structure. For example, one structure for the real numbers without multiplication would have signature $(0,1,+)$, and domain ${\mathbb{R}}$, the set of reals. The subset ${\mathbb{R}}$ given by all elements strictly less than $2$, $\{x:x<2\}$, is not definable in that structure. However, if we expand the signature to $(0,1,+,\cdot)$, then that set is definable, as $\{x:\exists y\;(y\cdot y)+x=2\}$.

A structure ${\mathcal{B}}$ is an **expansion** of a structure ${\mathcal{A}}$ if their domains are the same, but the signature of ${\mathcal{A}}$ is a subset of the signature of ${\mathcal{B}}$. Alternatively, we say that ${\mathcal{A}}$ is a **reduct** of ${\mathcal{B}}$. We use the terms “proper expansion” and “proper reduct” accordingly. If ${\mathcal{B}}$ is an expansion of ${\mathcal{A}}$, then the collection of definable sets in ${\mathcal{B}}$ is a superset of the collection of definable sets in ${\mathcal{A}}$.

Here we extend this concept from structures to theories, to say that a theory $T_{B}$ is a **(proper) expansion** of a theory $T_{A}$ if the set of extra-logical symbols occurring in the sentences in $T_{A}$ is a (proper) subset of those occurring in the sentences in $T_{B}$, and in addition, $T_{A}$ is a (proper) subset of $T_{B}$. We say that the expansion is consistent if $T_{B}$ is consistent, and finite if $T_{B}\setminus T_{A}$ is finite.

If a theory $T$ is both (syntactically) complete and consistent (and so does not include Peano arithmetic), then by definition, all structures that satisfy that theory are elementarily equivalent. (This does not mean that they are isomorphic in general however, as discussed above.) On the other hand, if $T$ is not complete, then in general there will be pairs of structures that separately satisfy $T$ but are not elementarily equivalent, disagreeing with one another about the truth of one or more sentences not contained in either $T$ or the set of logical consequences of $T$

These issues will be important below, when we introduce “auxiliary constraints”, and then later, when we introduce “stochastic emergence”.

## Interpretability between structures and between theories

The central concept of this paper is interpretability. Intuitively, interpretability involves a “translation”, defining the functions, relations and constants of the first structure (resp., theory) in terms of formulas in the second structure (resp., theory).

We first present the formal definition of this concept for the systematic construction of one structure inside another that has a different signature. We next extend this to defining the systematic construction of one theory inside another one that has a different signature.

### Interpretation of structures

**Definition 3.1** (Interpretation of structures). Let $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ be structures in signatures $\Sigma_{A}$ and $\Sigma_{B}$, having domains $A$ and $B$, respectively. A $k$**-dimensional interpretation** of $\mathcal{A}$ in $\mathcal{B}$ consists of: ^def-3-1

- (i) A domain formula $\Delta(\bar{x})$ in $\mathcal{L}_{B}$ with $k$ free variables, picking out $\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq B^{k}$.
- (ii) A formula $E^{\mathcal{B}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})$ in $\mathcal{L}_{B}$ which is an equivalence relation on $\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}$. The associated equivalence classes are written as $\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}/E^{\mathcal{B}}$.
- (iii) For each $n$-ary relation symbol $R\in\Sigma_{A}$, a formula $\varphi_{R}$ in $\mathcal{L}_{B}$, defining a relation on $\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}/E^{\mathcal{B}}$ invariant under $E^{\mathcal{B}}$.
- (iv) For each $n$-ary function symbol $f\in\Sigma_{A}$, a formula $\varphi_{f}$ in $\mathcal{L}_{B}$, defining a function on $\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}/E^{\mathcal{B}}$ invariant under $E^{\mathcal{B}}$.
- (v) For each constant symbol $c\in\Sigma_{A}$, a formula $\varphi_{c}$ in $\mathcal{L}_{B}$, picking out an $E^{\mathcal{B}}$-class.
- (vi) The induced structure $\mathcal{A}^{*}_{\mathcal{B}}:=(\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}/E^{\mathcal{B}},\,\varphi_{R}^{\mathcal{B}},\,\varphi_{f}^{\mathcal{B}},\,\varphi_{c}^{\mathcal{B}})$ must be isomorphic to $\mathcal{A}$.

In the sequel, we will typically leave the dimension $k$ implicit. We will sometimes refer to the structure $\mathcal{B}$ in [[#^def-3-1|Definition 3.1]] as the **lower** structure, with $\mathcal{A}$ being the **upper** structure.

The classic example of interpretation of structures (which has nothing to do with emergence) is how one can use a structure of integers to encode a structure of the rationals. It is illustrative to formulate this example using the concepts of computer programming.

**Example 3.1**. Consider an object-oriented programming language with a single type of variables, the integers. Suppose the programming language includes the the binary functions $+$ and $\times$ operating on the integers, together with the constants $0$ and $1$ and an infinite set of parentheses, but no other functions or constants. Within this programming language, we will define a new object class that we call “Rational” This object has two integers as private variables, $a$ and $b$, with $b\neq 0$. Intuitively, we will identify any two objects in this class whose private variable pairs $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ are related by $a\times d=b\times c$ to be equivalent objects, as far as any code using this object class is concerned. To do this, first adopt the convention that $(a,b)$ is shorthand for an object of class Rational whose two private variables are $a$ and $b$. Given this shorthand, define a public method for addition of two objects of the rational class, and a public method for multiplication of two objects in class Rational: ^ex-3-1

$$
\begin{align}
& PLUS[(a,b),(c,d)]=(a\times d+b\times c,\;b\times d) \label{eq:2} \\
& TIMES[(a,b),(c,d)]=(a\times c,\;b\times d) \label{eq:3}
\end{align}
$$

In other words, addition and multiplication of Rationals are overloaded binary functions specified in the object class definition, whose arguments are pairs of integers.

This is an example of interpretation, of the structure of the rational numbers, with signature ${\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{Q}}=\{0,1,\times,+\}$ and domain ${\mathbb{Q}}$, into the structure of the integers, with signature ${\mathcal{L}}_{\mathbb{Z}}=\{0,1,\times,+\}$ and domain ${\mathbb{Z}}$, where $k=2$.[^authors-4] The equivalence relation $E^{{\mathcal{B}}}(\cdot,\cdot)$ from [[#^def-3-1|Definition 3.1(ii)]] is the set of pairs of integers modulo the formula $a\times d=b\times c$. The interpretation of the two binary functions in the signature of the rationals into formulas in the signature of the integers is given in Eqs. $\eqref{eq:2}$ and $\eqref{eq:3}$. The interpretation of the constants in the signature of the rationals is simply the associated constants in the signature of the integers. It is straightforward to verify that these formulas respect the equivalence relation $E^{{\mathcal{B}}}(\cdot,\cdot)$, and that the structure induced by this equivalence relation and those formulas is isomorphic to the structure of the rationals.

### Interpreting theories

The definition of interpreting one structure inside another can be extended to define the interpretation of one theory inside another. The general concept is illustrated graphically in Figure [[#^fig-2|2]], and is formalized as the following pair of definitions:

**Definition 3.2**. (Translations between theories) A $k$-dimensional **translation** from a theory $T_{A}$ in language $\mathcal{L}_{A}$ into a theory $T_{B}$ in language $\mathcal{L}_{B}$ is a tuple $\Gamma=(\Delta^{{B}},E^{{B}},\{\varphi_{s}\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}})$, where ^def-3-2

1. $\Delta^{{B}}$ is an ${\mathcal{L}}_{B}$-definable relation with $k$ arguments;
2. $E^{{B}}$ is an ${\mathcal{L}}_{B}$-definable relation with $2k$ arguments;
3. Every $\varphi_{s}$ is a formula in $\mathcal{L}_{B}$.

With abuse of notation, we sometimes write $\varphi_{s}$ as $\Gamma(s)$

Suppose we have a translation $\Gamma=(\Delta^{{B}},E^{{B}},\{\varphi_{s}\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}})$, and that $T_{B}$ proves that $E^{{B}}$ is an equivalence relation on $\Delta^{{B}}$, i.e., on all $k$-tuples that satisfy $\Delta^{{B}}$. Suppose as well that every $\varphi_{s}$ is **invariant** within each equivalence class in $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$, i.e. that for any $\varphi_{s}$, and every pair of models of $T_{B}$, $\mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{B}^{\prime}$, $\mathcal{B}\models\varphi_{s}$ iff $\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\models\varphi_{s}$. Then any model $\mathcal{B}$ of $T_{B}$ maps $\Delta^{{B}}$ into a definable subset of the domain of that specific model, $\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}$. Similarly, it maps $E^{{B}}$ into an equivalence relation over $\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}$, which we write as $E^{\mathcal{B}}$. Furthermore, that model maps each $\varphi_{s}$ into a formula that is invariant over the quotient space $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$. So $\mathcal{B}$ induces a structure, whose signature can be identified with ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$.

This allows us to make the following definition:

**Definition 3.3** (Interpretation of theories). A theory $T_{A}$ is **interpretable** in a theory $T_{B}$ if there is a translation $\Gamma=(\Delta^{{B}},E^{{B}},\{\varphi_{s}\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}})$ from $T_{A}$ into $T_{B}$ such that: ^def-3-3

- (i) $T_{B}$ proves that $E^{{B}}$ is an equivalence relation on $\Delta^{{B}}$;
- (ii) $T_{B}$ proves that every $\varphi_{s}$ is invariant within each equivalence class in $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$;
- (iii) For every $\mathcal{B}\models T_{B}$, the associated induced structure on $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$ is a model of $T_{A}$.

We write $T_{A}\trianglelefteq T_{B}$ if $T_{A}$ is interpretable in $T_{B}$, and refer to $T_{B}$ as the **lower** theory, with $T_{A}$ the **upper** theory.

In the sequel, we will typically leave the dimension $k$ implicit.

Interpretability preserves logical consequence in one direction:

$$
\begin{equation}
T_{A}\models s\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_{B}\models\Gamma(\varphi). \label{eq:4}
\end{equation}
$$

The converse fails in general. To see this intuitively, note that strengthening $T_{B}$ by adding axioms shrinks $\mathrm{Mod}(T_{B})$, thereby making more sentences provable from $T_{B}$, without changing what is provable from $T_{A}$. If $\eqref{eq:4}$ were a biconditional, adding axioms to the lower theory could force new consequences in the upper theory. When the biconditional does hold, the interpretation is called **faithful**.

Two theories are **mutually interpretable** if each is interpretable in the other. It is straightforward to confirm that interpretability is a transitive relation. Therefore mutual interpretability is an equivalence relation. We also say that two theories are **bi-interpretable** if their composition is isomorphic to the identity.

The equivalence classes under mutual interpretability form the **lattice of interpretability degrees**, a dense, distributive partial order that contains incomparable pairs. It’s important to note though that in his “linearity conjecture”, Friedman argues that if we restrict attention to “natural” mathematical theories, then this lattice collapses to a total order[^cite-wk-16].

### Microstates and interpretation

It can be useful to be able to measure the “size” of the set of elements in any equivalence class $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$. In the case of interpreting one structure inside another, there is no problem with doing this, and the set of elements within any particular equivalence class in $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$ is perfectly well-defined. This reflects the fact that the lower structure comes with a pre-defined set of states, its domain. In contrast, lower theories do not come with such a pre-defined domain. This means that in the case of interpreting one theory inside another, there is no *a priori* definition of the “set of elements within any particular equivalence class in $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$”. So we cannot define the number of elements in such a set, nor define a measure over such a set.

To circumvent this issue, let $R$ and $R^{\prime}$ be any two equivalence relations defined by two formulas in a language ${\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{B}}$, which we write as $\varphi_{R}$ and $\varphi_{R^{\prime}}$, respectively. Assume also that $T_{B}$ can prove that both of those formulas are equivalence relations. Write the associated equivalence classes as the sets $\{r\}$ and $\{r^{\prime}\}$, respectively. Then the formula $\varphi_{R}\land\varphi_{R^{\prime}}$ is also an equivalence relation, with its equivalence classes given by the set of all intersections of the two relations’ equivalence classes, $\{r\cap r^{\prime}\}$. Similarly, $\varphi_{R}\vee\varphi_{R^{\prime}}$ is an equivalence relation, with its equivalence classes given by the set of all unions of the two relation’s equivalence classes, $\{r\cup r^{\prime}\}$. Moreover, every equivalence class defined by $\varphi_{R}$ is a subset of an equivalence class defined by $\varphi_{R}\vee\varphi_{R^{\prime}}$, and every equivalence class defined by $\varphi_{R}\land\varphi_{R^{\prime}}$ is a subset of an equivalence class defined by $\varphi_{R}$. Therefore we can use subset inclusion to define a partial order over any set of equivalence classes all of which are both: definable by $T_{B}$; can be proven by $T_{B}$ to be equivalence classes.

Suppose we have such a set of equivalence relations, $\mathscr{R}$, and that the associated partial order is a finite lattice, with a maximally refined meet, defined / proven by a finite formula that we write as $\bigwedge_{R\in\mathscr{R}}\varphi_{R}$. We sometimes refer to the equivalence classes of this equivalence relation as **microstates**. On the other hand, we will refer to the equivalence classes of $\bigvee_{R\in\mathscr{R}}\,\varphi_{R}$ as the **macrostates** of $T_{B}$. Each of the equivalence classes $\Delta^{\mathcal{B}}/E^{\mathcal{B}}$ defined in [[#^def-3-3|Definition 3.3]] properly contains a distinct set of microstates. We will use this set of microstates as the analog, for the case of interpreting one theory in another, of the sets of states in each equivalence class that arise when interpreting one structure in another. The “size” of any macrostate is the cardinality of the set of all equivalence classes in that macrostate.

### General comments on interpretability of theories

The conditions in [[#^def-3-3|Definition 3.3]] that require $T_{B}$ to prove the translation $\Gamma$ is well-behaved on the quotient $\Delta^{B}/E^{B}$ involves three distinct requirements, each of which must be a logical consequence of $T_{B}$.

The first requirement is that $E^{B}$ must be an equivalence relation on $\Delta^{B}$. The formula $E^{B}(\bar{x},\bar{y})$ defining that equivalence relation has $2k$ free variables and provides a binary relation on $k$-tuples satisfying $\Delta^{{B}}$. For this relation to be an equivalence relation specifically, $T_{B}$ must prove reflexivity ($\forall\bar{x}\,[\Delta^{{B}}(\bar{x})\to E^{{B}}(\bar{x},\bar{x})]$), symmetry ($\forall\bar{x}\,\bar{y}\,[E^{{B}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\to E^{{B}}(\bar{y},\bar{x})]$), and transitivity ($\forall\bar{x}\,\bar{y}\,\bar{z}\,[E^{{B}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\land E^{{B}}(\bar{y},\bar{z})\to E^{{B}}(\bar{x},\bar{z})]$). An arbitrary formula with $2k$ free variables need not define an equivalence relation, and $T_{B}$ must force it to.

The second requirement is that each translated symbol $\varphi_{s}$ respects $E^{{B}}$. If two $k$-tuples are $E^{{B}}$-equivalent, (they represent the same macroscopic state), then substituting one for the other in any $\varphi_{s}$ must not change the truth value. Without this, the emergent operations would depend on which microscopic representative one happened to examine rather than on the equivalence class, and the macroscopic description would leak microscopic information. In the programming example, this says that if two memory configurations encode the same integer, then applying the compiled addition routine to either configuration must yield results that are themselves $E^{{B}}$-equivalent.

The third requirement is that the images of the translated function symbols are restricted to $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$, and in fact for every input class in $\Delta^{B}/E^{B}$, the function must produce an output class that is also in $\Delta^{B}/E^{B}$. This ensures the induced structure is closed under its own operations.

Because all three of these requirements must be proved by $T_{B}$, they hold in every model of $T_{B}$, not just in some conveniently chosen one. In the example of [[#From Languages and Machine Code to Hardware|Section 4]] below, a compiler that produces correct code on one chip but not another has not achieved interpretability. The translation must work across all chips.

If an interpretation from $T_{A}$ into $T_{B}$ is non-faithful, and $T_{B}$ has at least two models, then there are models of $T_{B}$ that disagree on $T_{A}$, or more generally (assuming $T_{A}$ is consistent) disagree on some logical consequences of $T_{A}$.[^authors-5] Therefore so long as $T_{B}$ has more than one model, a necessary condition for the interpretation to be non-faithful is that $T_{A}$ is not syntactically complete. When $T_{A}$ is not syntactically complete there are sentences $\phi$ in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ that are not logical consequences of $T_{A}$, and two models on $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$ will disagree on some of those sentences.

**Example 3.2**. As an illustration of such non-faithfulness, suppose that $T_{A}$ involves two functions $f,g$ that separately obey the same axioms, but no sentences in $T_{A}$ say how those two functions are related. Then the interpretation might result in induced structures on $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$ that don’t distinguish between those two functions, and therefore disagree with one another concerning sentences in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ that do distinguish those two functions. In this case $T_{A}$ is not syntactically complete. ^ex-3-2

As an explicit example, consider a “two-product group” theory $T_{A}$, with two binary operations $f,g$ and the four axioms:

$$
\begin{align}
& \exists e\,:\,\forall x,f(x,e)\;=\;x \label{eq:5} \\
& \exists e\,:\,\forall x,g(x,e)\;=\;x \label{eq:6} \\
& \forall x\exists y\;:\;f(x,y)\;=\;e \label{eq:7} \\
& \forall x\exists y\;:\;g(x,y)\;=\;e \label{eq:8}
\end{align}
$$

$T_{B}$ might include an additional axiom specifying that over $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$ that obeys the formula

$$
\begin{align}
& \forall x,\forall y\,f(x,y)=g(x,y) \label{eq:9}
\end{align}
$$

However, $T_{A}$ does not include that axiom, and there are both models of $T_{A}$ that satisfy it and models that don’t. So the interpretation is non-faithful and $T_{A}$ is incomplete.

Recall that Peano arithmetic is fundamental to almost all scientific theories, but is not syntactically complete, by Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem. However, this does not mean that any theory $T_{A}$ must by syntactically incomplete if it can be interpreted into another theory $T_{B}$ which includes the axioms of Peano arithmetic. The fact that $T_{B}$ is incomplete means that there are sentences in ${\mathcal{L}}_{B}$ on which two distinct models of $T_{B}$ disagree. However, those sentence might not be expressible as formulas in $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$, the projection of the signature of $T_{A}$ into $T_{B}$. So their disagreement may not back-project into an associated disagreement in $T_{A}$.

These considerations will be central to the some of the extensions of emergence we introduce below.

## From Languages and Machine Code to Hardware

The denotational semantics of a minimal, non-optimizing high-level programming language is an instance of model-theoretic interpretability of one theory into another.[^authors-6] Denotational semantics is used to verify that the meaning of source code and target code are identical. The results of compilation thus provide a natural bridge to the study of emergence[^cite-wk-22]. We develop this idea in detail because it grounds the abstractions introduced above in familiar mechanisms. Furthermore, the relationship between a target program and the hardware that executes it exemplifies the distinction between theory and structure. High level languages compiled down into machine code that configure time-varying hardware-states cover aspects of the ontological and epistemological factors described by Nagel, Tarski and Bunge as well as scientifically motivated definitions.

### Two signatures, two languages

Consider a minimal high-level language $H$ and a machine-level language $M$. The signature $\Sigma_{H}$ of $H$ contains a sort for integers, a sort for Booleans, function symbols for arithmetic ($+_{H},\times_{H}$), a conditional function symbol $\mathsf{if}$, and constant symbols $\mathsf{true},\mathsf{false},0_{H},1_{H}$. The signature $\Sigma_{M}$ of $M$ contains a sort for bit-strings of length $w$ (words), function symbols for bitwise AND, OR, NOT, addition modulo $2^{w}$, and a shift operator, and a constant symbol $\mathbf{0}$ (the zero word).

These signatures share no non-logical symbols. The language $\mathcal{L}_{H}$ can express statements like $\forall x\,(x+_{H}0_{H}=x)$, and the language $\mathcal{L}_{M}$ can express statements like $\forall v\,(\mathsf{AND}(v,\mathbf{0})=\mathbf{0})$. Neither language can directly state sentences in the other’s vocabulary.

Let $T_{H}$ be the high-level theory, the $\mathcal{L}_{H}$-sentences derivable from the axioms that define the high-level language, equivalently the properties that hold of every well-formed high-level program. Let $T_{M}$ be the machine theory, the $\mathcal{L}_{M}$-sentences derivable from the axioms of the machine. In practice, the first thing a compiler does with a program is check that each of its lines is a syntactically valid sentence of $\mathcal{L}_{H}$, that is, that the program belongs to the language whose theory is $T_{H}$.

### Interpretation as denotational translation

The denotational translation underlying compilation interprets one theory in another. This definitional translation provides three components required by Definition [[#^def-3-3|3.3]].

The first is a domain formula, and the integers of $H$ are encoded as equivalence classes of bit-strings in $M$. For unsigned integers up to $2^{w}-1$ the encoding is one-dimensional, with each integer mapping to one word, while for arbitrary-precision integers it is multi-dimensional, with each integer mapping to a tuple of words representing a linked sequence of digits.

The second is an equivalence relation, and two bit-strings are equivalent when they encode the same integer. For fixed-width arithmetic this is just bitwise equality, while for arbitrary-precision representations it is the relation “these two linked lists represent the same number.”

The third is a definable translation of each symbol. The high-level $+_{H}$ is translated into a formula in $\mathcal{L}_{M}$ that chains together word-level additions with carry propagation. The conditional $\mathsf{if}$ is translated into a formula involving branch-on-zero. Each translation is a formula in $\mathcal{L}_{M}$, definable from the primitives of $M$, invariant under the equivalence relation.

This translation constructs, inside any model $\mathcal{M}$ of the machine-level theory $T_{M}$, an induced structure on $\Delta^{\mathcal{M}}/E^{\mathcal{M}}$ that is itself a model of the high-level theory $T_{H}$. Because this holds for every model of $T_{M}$ and is provable from $T_{M}$, we have $T_{H}\trianglelefteq T_{M}$.

### The theory-structure distinction and hardware as model

The theory $T_{M}$ specifies how the machine-level operations behave, how addition modulo $2^{w}$ is associative and commutative, AND distributes over OR, and so on. These are sentences and abstract constraints. and they say nothing about voltage levels, clock speeds, or silicon.

A particular processor (an Intel chip, an ARM core, an FPGA configured to implement the same instruction set) is a structure $\mathcal{M}$ satisfying $T_{M}$. Different processors are different structures satisfying the same theory. They are elementarily equivalent (they agree on all first-order sentences about word-level operations) but they are not isomorphic, and they differ in physical properties (power consumption, heat dissipation, propagation delay) that are not expressible in $\mathcal{L}_{M}$.

The same program runs on different hardware. The program is a consequence of the theory and the execution is a process in a structure. The theory constrains the logic and the structure embodies the logic. When we say that the high-level language “emerges from” the machine code, we are making a claim at the level of theories ($T_{H}\trianglelefteq T_{M}$), not at the level of structures. Different chips give different structures, but the interpretability relation holds uniformly across all of them, because it is proved from $T_{M}$, not from any particular model. What holds for all models of a theory versus what holds in a particular model is the difference between emergence and instantiation.

The interpretation map (the denotational translation) is one-directional. Every sentence provable in $T_{H}$ translates to a sentence provable in $T_{M}$. But different choices for $T_{M}$ all prove sentences that have no counterpart in $T_{H}$, among them overflow behavior and alignment constraints. The machine-level theory draws distinctions invisible at the high level. This is what it means to say the lower level is “richer,” and the notion can be made fully formal. Fix the translation $\Gamma$ with domain formula $\Delta$ and congruence $E$. The emergent theory $T_{H}$ can speak only of $E$-invariant sets, and a subset of $\Delta$ is visible to $T_{H}$ exactly when it is a union of $E$-classes of the form $\Gamma(\psi)$ for some $\mathcal{L}_{H}$-formula $\psi$. To say that $T_{M}$ is richer than $T_{H}$ is to say that some $\mathcal{L}_{M}$-definable subset of $\Delta$ is not a union of $E$-classes, but splits at least one class. Overflow behavior, alignment, and the like are exactly such $\mathcal{L}_{M}$-definable predicates, separating memory layouts that lie inside a single integer-class and so are indistinguishable in $\mathcal{L}_{H}$. A strictly stronger reading is available through faithfulness, and $\Gamma$ is faithful when $T_{H}\models\varphi$ if and only if $T_{M}\models\Gamma(\varphi)$, while $T_{M}$ is richer when $T_{H}\trianglelefteq T_{M}$ holds but no interpretation between them is faithful, so that $T_{M}$ settles translated sentences that $T_{H}$ leaves open[^cite-wk-40].

Conversely, $T_{H}$ may contain constructs (garbage collection, dynamic typing, closures) that require elaborate construction from the primitives of $T_{M}$. The higher level is “concise” in the sense that short sentences in $\mathcal{L}_{H}$ compress long formulas in $\mathcal{L}_{M}$. Furthermore, the translation is computable but may involve exponential blow-up in formula length. We return to this issue in Section [[#Computational complexity and the limits of interpretability|8]].

## Emergence

### Emergence as constrained interpretability

An important feature of the compiler example is that $T_{H}$ is interpretable in $T_{M}$ outright, and no additional axioms are needed. In scientific practice the situation is different.

The ideal gas law is not interpretable in Newtonian mechanics alone since one must add typicality conditions on initial configurations, e.g., restricting to systems with large particle numbers, requiring all collisions be perfectly elastic, defining the shape of the enclosing container, etc. The emergent theory ($PV=NT$) becomes interpretable only in the enriched theory $T_{\text{Newton}}\cup C$, where $C$ encodes these physical constraints as sentences in the lower theory of Newtonian mechanics.

**Definition 5.1** (Emergence). Let $T_{A}$ and $T_{B}$ be two theories, and let $\widetilde{T_{B}}=T_{B}\cup C$ for a finite, consistent set $C$ of sentences of $\mathcal{L}_{B}$, such that $T_{A}\trianglelefteq\widetilde{T_{B}}$. We then say that $T_{A}$ **emerges from** $T_{B}$ **using auxiliary constraints** $C=\widetilde{T_{B}}\setminus T_{B}$. ^def-5-1

Whenever $\widetilde{T_{B}}\setminus T_{B}=\varnothing$, emergence reduces to interpretability. More generally, adding auxiliary constraints restricts the class of models under consideration. From among all systems obeying $T_{B}$, they select those that exhibit the emergent regularity.

Of course, rather than use [[#^def-5-1|Definition 5.1]] one could instead simply say that $T_{A}$ is interpretable in $\widetilde{T_{B}}$. The benefit of [[#^def-5-1|Definition 5.1]] is that it allows us to capture the case where multiple theories $\{T_{i}\}$ all emerge from the same theory $T_{B}$, but each using different auxiliary constraints, where those auxiliary constraints are not consistent with one another. In this case there is no single theory $T$ such all of the $\{T_{i}\}$ are interpretable in $T$, even though they all emerge from the same theory. For example, both the laws of thermodynamics and those of mechanical engineering emerge from Newtonian mechanics, even though neither is interpretable in Newtonian mechanics.[^authors-7]

If $T_{A}$ is interpretable in $T_{B}$ and $T_{B}$ emerges from $T_{C}$, then $T_{A}$ emerges from $T_{C}$. So emergence without constraints is transitive. However, suppose instead that $T_{A}$ emerges from $T_{B}$, while $T_{B}$ is interpretable in $T_{C}$. In this case we cannot conclude that $T_{A}$ emerges from $T_{C}$. The problem is that the auxiliary constraints added to $T_{B}$ (to allow $T_{A}$ to emerge from $T_{B}$) might not themselves be interpretable in $T_{C}$.

Note that there are several different ways that $T_{A}$ might fail to be interpretable in $T_{B}$ if we don’t add the auxiliary constraints. Any of the three parts of [[#^def-3-3|Definition 3.3]] might fail, even though they hold if we expand $T_{B}$ by adding the sentences in $\widetilde{T_{B}}\setminus T_{B}$. Note as well that $T_{A}$ may emerge non-faithfully from $T_{B}$, if $T_{A}$ is not syntactically complete. This is the starting point for the concept of “stochastic emergence” discussed below.

There are numerous ways that we must augment this core definition of emergence in [[#^def-5-1|Definition 5.1]] to capture all the ways the term is used in the sciences and philosophy. For example, classical genetics as emergent from chemistry, discussed in Section [[#Insufficiency of interpretations with constraints|5.3]], lies at the far end of this spectrum from the thermodynamics as emergent from Newton’s laws. Genetics cannot be made interpretable in chemistry by adding a set of auxiliary constraints $C$. The reason is simple: the sentences in any such $C$ are drawn from $\mathcal{L}_{\text{chem}}$, and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{chem}}$ does not contain the extra-logical symbols that are needed to capture the intrinsically statistical nature of the higher theory of genetics. The lower theory must itself be augmented with additional mathematical properties to capture this statistical nature of the higher theory. This is elaborated below in [[#Probabilistic Emergence|Section 6.1]] and [[#Stochastic Emergence|Section 6.3]].

### Richness, expressivity, and parsimony

The preceding definitions provide several quantities that are key to the model theoretic framing of emergence, namely the dimension $k$ of the interpretation, the size of the equivalence classes under $E$, and the expansion of each extra-logical symbol in the upper language into a string of symbols in the lower language. Together they determine the parsimony of the emergent theory relative to the lower theory.

Consider two structures $\mathcal{A}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ with signatures $\Sigma_{A}$ and $\Sigma_{B}$, respectively, along with domains $A$ and $B$. Suppose that in addition $\mathcal{A}$ emerges from $\mathcal{B}$ using a specified $k$-dimensional translation map. The dimension $k$ of that translation is the number of $B$-coordinates required to represent a single element of $A$. In the interpretation of $(\mathbb{Q},+,\times,0,1)$ in $(\mathbb{Z},+,\times,0,1)$, we have $k=2$ where each rational requires a pair of integers. This example concerns the interpretation of one structure in another rather than one theory in another. The theory-level version, which requires axiomatizing both the rationals and the integers, is the standard construction carried out in the model-theory literature[^cite-wk-17].

As another example, in the emergence of the ideal gas law from Newtonian mechanics, each macrostate (a quadruple $(N,P,V,T)$) is encoded by a configuration of $N$ particles in a $6N$-dimensional phase space, so $k$ is proportional to the number of particles. The sentence “$P=3$” in the emergent theory, which names a single macroscopic quantity, translates via $\Gamma$ into a formula over $k$-tuples of phase-space coordinates. We say that the dimension $k$ measures the **width** of the translation, and it counts how many microscopic degrees of freedom are bundled into one emergent (extra-logical) symbol.

Suppose that the equivalence class $\bigvee_{R}\,\varphi_{R}$ discussed at the end of [[#Interpreting theories|Section 3.2]] is well-defined and finite. So the number of microstates within each equivalence class in $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$ is well-defined and finite. In such a case, the equivalence classes $[b]_{E}$ defining $\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}$ provide a different measure of compression. Every sentence of $T_{A}$, when translated via $\Gamma$, is invariant over these classes, and it cannot distinguish microstates within a single class. A large equivalence class means that many $k$-tuples in $B^{k}$ are identified as representing the same emergent state.

The number of distinctions the emergent theory can draw is bounded by $|\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}|$, which is smaller than $|\Delta^{{B}}|$ by a factor equal to the average class size. Large classes mean the emergent theory is blind to most microscopic detail. Assuming some appropriate measure over the number of equivalence classes in $|\Delta^{{B}}/E^{{B}}|$, we refer to this expected number of microstates in each equivalence class as the **depth** of the translation, and write it as $\overline{|[b]_{E}|}$. An obvious variant is to instead consider the maximum over all equivalence classes of the number of microstates per class, assuming that is well-defined. Yet another variant arises when probability measures are defined using an extension of $\mathcal{B}$, as described below in [[#^def-6-1|Definition 6.1]]. In such a situation the probability measure allows us to dispense with microstates entirely.

Width and depth characterize features of the translation’s map between the domains of two structures that satisfy $T_{A}$ and $T_{B}$, respectively. However, the translation also involves a map between the signatures of those two theories. Each extra-logical symbol $\alpha\in\Sigma_{A}$ gets mapped by the translation into a formula $\varphi_{\alpha}$, which consists of a string of (logical and extra-logical) symbols which has length $|\varphi_{\alpha}|$. So the maximum over all $\alpha\in\Sigma_{A}$ of $|\varphi_{\alpha}|$ is a measure of how much the symbols in the upper theory compress symbols in the lower theory. We call that maximum the **blow-up** of the translation, and write it as $\widehat{\{\varphi_{s}\}}$.

Parsimony in the emergent theory $T_{A}$ relative to $T_{B}$ is the joint effect of all of these quantities. When $k$ is large, each emergent concept encodes many microscopic degrees of freedom. When the equivalence classes are large, many microstates that could be distinguished using the theory $T_{B}$ are treated as identical. Finally, when the blow-up is large, each symbol in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ represents many symbols in ${\mathcal{L}}_{B}$. The product of these types of compression is

$$
k\;\cdot\;\overline{|[b]_{E}|}\;\cdot\;\widehat{\{\varphi_{s}\}}
$$

(dimension times average class size times blow-up factor) gives a rough measure of the compression ratio, and counts how many microscopic degrees of freedom are collapsed into one emergent degree of freedom. A high compression ratio is what makes the emergent theory so efficient since a short sentence in $\mathcal{L}_{A}$ encodes, via $\Gamma$, a long and complicated formula in $\mathcal{L}_{B}$.

This also gives a formal criterion for the asymmetry between “richer” and “expressive” that the programming example illustrated informally. A theory $T_{B}$ is richer than $T_{A}$ when $T_{A}$ is interpretable in $T_{B}$ but not vice versa. When there is that asymmetry, there exist $\mathcal{L}_{B}$-definable sets in models of $T_{B}$ that are not unions of $E$-classes, and they cut across the classes, distinguishing microstates that the emergent theory cannot. The sets the emergent theory can name are exactly the $E$-invariant ones, namely the $\Gamma$-images of $\mathcal{L}_{A}$-definable sets, and the distinctions that $T_{B}$ draws and $T_{A}$ cannot are precisely those $\mathcal{L}_{B}$-definable sets that fail to be $E$-invariant.

The emergent theory $T_{A}$ is more expressive in the complementary sense, and it can state, in a short sentence using an appropriate vocabulary, a regularity that in $\mathcal{L}_{B}$ requires quantification over all elements of a large equivalence class. Expressivity provides both a compression and a unified mechanism as encoded in signatures and sentences, whereas richness describes the resolution. They are related by the interpretation map as two sides of the same asymmetry.

In the compiler example, the high-level sentence “$x+y=z$” compresses a formula in $\mathcal{L}_{M}$ that involves carry propagation across $w$ bit positions, overflow checking, and so forth. The compression is possible because the equivalence relation $E$ identifies all memory layouts that encode the same integer and the dimension $k$ bundles $w$ bits into one high-level value. The emergent theory (the high-level language) is more concise than the lower theory (machine code) in the sense that it says the same thing in fewer symbols, but the lower theory is richer in the sense that it can distinguish many memory layouts that the high-level theory cannot describe. The same logic of compression and resolution applies to coarse graining, multiple-realizability and symmetry breaking since all of them express the value of effective theories in terms of their advantages beyond detailed descriptions whose partitions are not easily observed.

### Insufficiency of interpretations with constraints

The interpretation of the structure of the rationals in the integers is a relation between two fixed structures, and its criterion of success is an isomorphism. Interpreting one theory in another is different as per Definition [[#^def-3-3|3.3]], avoiding the need for isomorphism. Indeed, there is no single target structure to be isomorphic to. The requirement is that every model of the lower theory must induce, through one uniform translation, a model of the upper theory.

Classical genetics in relation to chemistry is an example of this difference between interpretation among structures and interpretation among theories. It also shows why plain interpretability is not enough, with or without auxiliary constraints.

Let $T_{\text{gen}}$ be the theory of classical Mendelian genetics, with a many-sorted signature of a certain number of genes, alleles, loci, genotypes, and the relations of dominance and segregation. Let $T_{\text{chem}}$ be a theory of chemistry, with a many-sorted signature of atoms, bonds, and molecular configurations, along with unary functions taking values like hydrophobicity, polarity, charge, etc., and other functions specifying stoichiometrically allowed reactions, etc. The two signatures share no non-logical symbols. Moreover, a single allele corresponds to an enormous configuration of atoms, so the domain formula $\Delta$ would select large tuples of chemical variables and the congruence $E$ would group those configurations into alleles of genes.

Strict interpretation into $T_{\text{chem}}$ fails immediately. Mendel’s law of segregation, under which the two alleles at a locus separate into gametes in equal proportion so that a monohybrid cross yields offspring in a $3\!:\!1$ ratio, is not a theorem of chemistry. No sentence of $T_{\text{chem}}$ proves it, because the ratio is a consequence of the mechanics and combinatorics of chromosome pairing at meiosis, not of the chemistry of any molecule. Thus $T_{\text{gen}}\not\trianglelefteq T_{\text{chem}}$. This is a typical situation where the vocabulary of an emergent discipline is not a coarse-graining of the vocabulary of the theory it emerges from.

Adding constraints does not repair this. One might hope to adjoin to $T_{\text{chem}}$ a set $C$ of constraints encoding diploidy and the meiotic apparatus and thereby obtain $T_{\text{gen}}\trianglelefteq T_{\text{chem}}\cup C$. The obstruction is that definition [[#^def-3-3|3.3]] requires $E$ to be $\mathcal{L}_{\text{chem}}$-definable and each symbol of $\Sigma_{\text{gen}}$ to be sent to a single fixed formula of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{chem}}$ that $T_{\text{chem}}\cup C$ proves invariant. Neither requirement holds here. The equivalence that genetics needs is sameness of allele up to phenotypic effect, and phenotypic effect reaches downstream into a developmental and environmental cascade that lies outside the chemistry of the DNA molecule itself, so $E$ is not definable in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{chem}}$ and cannot be made so by adding sentences to the set of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{chem}}$-axioms. The characteristic relations are context-sensitive for the same reason. Whether an allele is dominant, and with what expressivity, depends on genetic background, modifier loci, and environment, so there is no fixed, context-free formula $\varphi_{\text{dom}}$ of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{chem}}$ into which the dominance symbol could be translated. This is exactly the compiler’s failure. A construct whose meaning depends on its whole context is not sent to a single formula, and a congruence that is not definable in the lower language cannot be recovered by strengthening the lower theory.

The contrast with the compiler is instructive. There, a stripped-down, non-optimizing high-level language had a core that was interpretable in the same lower theory $T_{M}$. Genetics has no such core and nothing in it is interpretable in chemistry alone, whether or not chemistry is constrained. Its interpretable core appears only after the lower theory is expanded.

This expansion is formalized in [[#Extensions of Emergence|Section 6]] below as “multiple emergence”. Genetics emerges jointly from chemistry, from a theory of chromosomal mechanics and the combinatorics of segregation, which is where the $3\!:\!1$ ratio comes from since it is a counting statistic rather than a chemical mechanism, and from a developmental and environmental theory. In the composite language of these coupled basis theories the genetic sorts and the congruence $E$ become definable, and an idealized, deterministic Mendelian core becomes interpretable in the multiple lower theory in the sense of Definition [[#^def-6-5|6.5]]. The distinctions genetics draws are $\mathcal{L}_{\text{chem}}$-invisible not because chemistry is too coarse but because they live in a language chemistry does not contain, and multiple emergence seeks to add in the missing language.

This is an important insight to emphasize. Genetics reduces to chemistry ontologically, in that every organism is a chemical system, while failing to be interpretable in chemistry epistemologically, even after chemistry is constrained, because its congruence and its characteristic relations are not constructible in the chemical language. Interpretability is regained only in an expanded, multiple lower theory. The statistical laws that give genetics its disciplinary content require, in addition, developing a measure over a larger space of models.

## Extensions of Emergence

Model theory is a very powerful tool for formalizing “emergence”, much more broadly applicable than any of the particular formalizations that have previously been investigated. Even so, there are many instances of emergence that it cannot capture. In this section we present several different extensions of model theory that allow us that capture far more instances of emergence.

An important property of several of these extensions is that they introduce probability measures into the theories. In the real world, outside of quantum mechanics, probability only arises due to uncertainty / ignorance on the part of the scientist building the models. Probabilities are not part of the intrinsic, physical processes. Accordingly, in the associated extensions of model theory below, the probability measures are all exogenous to the theories, to be specified by the scientist using these extensions of model theory. An obvious variant would be to instead require in the usual way that the probability measures (and associated sigma algebras) can be defined as functions, in the model-theoretic sense, in the relevant theories, and that those theories can prove that such functions are in fact proper probability measures. In the interests of space, we do not consider such variants here.

### Probabilistic Emergence

There are very many examples where one colloquially views a particular scientific discipline that involves probability distributions as “emerging” from another discipline, that also involves probability distributions. In particular, very often the distributions defined in the lower theory directly provide the distributions defined in the upper theory. We can capture this as follows.

Suppose that a theory $T_{A}$ emerges from a theory $T_{B}$, and so is interpretable into a theory $\widetilde{T_{B}}$ using translation $\Gamma=(\Delta,E,\{\varphi_{s}\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}})$. Suppose as well that ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ can be used to define a set $S_{A}$, and that each sentence in $T_{A}$ specifies that the variables in it must be from $S_{A}$.[^authors-8] Finally, suppose $T_{A}$ provides a sigma algebra over $S_{A}$, $\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}}$.

By [[#^def-3-3|Definition 3.3]], there are formulas in ${\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}$ that define the equivalence relation $\Delta/E$ and that say whether two associated equivalence classes are equal. In addition, it’s required that the theory $\widetilde{T_{B}}$ proves that those formulas define a proper equivalence relation. Moreover, we can apply $\Gamma$ to the (extra-logical symbols in the) formula in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ which defines $S_{A}$, to construct a bijection between $S_{A}$ and a subset of $\Delta/E$ that we write as $\Gamma(S_{A})$. Similarly, $\Gamma$ provides a sigma algebra isomorphism between $\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}}$ and a sigma algebra over $\Gamma(S_{A})$ that we write as $\Gamma(\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}})$.

Next, suppose we also have a sigma algebra $\mathscr{F}_{\widetilde{S_{B}}}$, defined using formulas over ${\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}$, and that $\mathscr{F}_{\widetilde{S_{B}}}$ is a refinement of the sigma algebra $\Gamma(\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}})$. Then any probability measure P${}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}$ defined over the events defined by the sigma algebra ${\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}$ specifies a unique measure P${}_{\Gamma(\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}})}$ over the events defined by $\Gamma(\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}})$. That measure in turn specifies a unique measure over $\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}}$.

In terms of our framework, P${}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}$ is a probability measure over the states of any physical system that obeys the scientific formalism $T_{B}$ when there are auxiliary constraints $\widetilde{T_{B}}\setminus T_{B}$. In turn, $\Gamma$ provides a way to uniquely map that measure to a probability measure over the states of certain physical systems that obey the scientific formalism $T_{A}$.

We summarize this with the following definition:

**Definition 6.1**. (Probabilistic Emergence) Suppose that a theory $T_{A}$ emerges from a theory $T_{B}$, using translation $\Gamma=(\Delta^{B},E^{B},\{\varphi_{s}\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}})$. Suppose as well that ^def-6-1

1. ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ can be used to define a set $S_{A}$ and a sigma algebra over that set, $\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}}$;
2. Each sentence in $T_{A}$ specifies that the variables in it must be from $S_{A}$;
3. ${\mathcal{L}}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}$ can be used to define a sigma algebra $\mathscr{F}_{\widetilde{S_{B}}}$ over $\Delta^{B}$, which is a refinement of the sigma algebra $\Gamma(\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}})$; ^def-6-1-3
4. We are given a probability measure P${}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}$ over the sigma algebra $\mathscr{F}_{\widetilde{S_{B}}}$. ^def-6-1-4

Finally, suppose as well that $\widetilde{T_{B}}$ proves both [[#^def-6-1-3|Item 3]] and [[#^def-6-1-4|Item 4]].

In this situation P${}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}$ specifies a probability measure P${}_{S_{A}}$ over the sigma algebra $\mathscr{F}_{S_{A}}$, and we say that $(T_{A},S_{A},\rm{P}_{S_{A}})$ **probabilistically emerges** from $(T_{B},\Gamma(S_{A}),\rm{P}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}})$ (using translation $\Gamma=(\Delta,E,\{\varphi_{s}\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}})$).

Probabilistic emergence provides a way to have a probability measure in a lower theory $\widetilde{T_{B}}$ generate a probability measure in an an upper theory $T_{A}$ that emerges from $T_{B}$. Loosely speaking, if we have a unary relation $R$ defined over ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$, then the distribution $P_{\Gamma(S_{A})}$ in the lower theory, induced by the distribution $\rm{P}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}$, tells us the probability that $R$ is true. Moreover, each equivalencxe class in $\Delta^{B}/E^{B}$ need not be an indivisible event of the sigma algebra $\mathscr{F}_{\widetilde{S_{B}}}$, and there might be multiple events in such an equivalence class, which need not have the same probability.

As an illustration, the Boltzmann distribution over the states of a finite system $C$ that is connected to an extremely large heat bath $D$ probabilistically emerges from the microcanonical distribution over the joint states of $C$ and $D$. We can use this, e.g., to evaluate the probability that the energy of the system exceeds some given constant.

In general, the definition of the sigma algebra $\mathscr{F}_{\widetilde{S_{B}}}$ might use sentences that cannot be constructed from the (appropriate quantifications of) the formulas that define $\Delta^{B}/E^{B}$. An example where that is often the case is ff there are microstates defined in both the lower theory and the upper theory, as discussed in [[#Interpreting theories|Section 3.2]], and we use those microstates to define the associated sigma algebras.

### Approximate Emergence

In many cases one formalism won’t exactly arise from another one, but only approximately do so, to within some accuracy. Typically such approximate emergence is combined with a probability measure over a sigma algebra defined using the upper formalism’s language. For example, often in gathering data we will have “experimental” or “observational” noise, that result in some “error bars” about the value of some observable in the upper formalism.

We can capture this scenario with the following extension of emergence:

**Definition 6.2**. (Approximate emergence) Suppose that $(T_{A},S_{A},\rm{P}_{S_{A}})$ probabilistically emerges from $(T_{B},\Gamma(S_{A}),\rm{P}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}})$, and that it does so using a translation that we write as $\Gamma=(\Delta^{B},E^{B},\{\varphi_{s}\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}})$, along with some implicit auxiliary constraints constructing $\widetilde{T_{B}}$ from $T_{B}$. Suppose as well that we have a metric $d^{B}(.,.)$ defined by appropriately combining the formulas $\{\Gamma(\varphi_{s})\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}}$. Suppose as well that ($\widetilde{T_{B}}$ proves that) $d^{B}(.,.)$ is invariant over the equivalence classes $E^{B}/\Delta^{B}$. In this situation, $\Gamma^{-1}(d^{B}(.,.))$ is a well-defined formula in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$, defining a distance measure $d^{A}(.,.)$. ^def-6-2

Then we say that $(T_{A},S_{A},{\rm{P}_{S_{A}}},d^{A})$ **approximately emerges** from the associated tuple $(T_{B},\Gamma(S_{A}),{\rm{P}_{\widetilde{T_{B}}}},d^{B})$ (using translation $\Gamma=(\Delta^{B},E^{B},\{\varphi_{s}\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}})$).

Note that the definition of approximate emergence implicitly assumes that there is a set of cardinal numbers, e.g., ${\mathbb{N}}$ or ${\mathbb{Z}}$, definable in both $\widetilde{T_{B}}$ and $T_{A}$. If this weren’t the case, then it would not be possible for the distance measures to have numerical values.

Approximate emergence provides a way for us to use a provided metric and probability measure over a lower theory to specify the probability distribution over the distances between any two elements (in the set $S_{A}$ defined) in the upper theory.

### Stochastic Emergence

Often in cases of what is called “emergence” of one scientific formalism from another, the laws themselves in the emergent formalism only hold with a certain probability. This behavior is not possible using conventional model-theoretic interpretation (assuming that the upper, emerging theory is consistent). Adding auxiliary constraints as in [[#^def-5-1|Definition 5.1]] doesn’t salvage the situation.

To see how to capture the possibility of a law in an upper theory only holding with a certain probability, note that in practice, in situations where we view a law in an emergent formalism only holding with some probability, what we really mean is that there is a certain probability of its holding in a physical system which satisfies the lower formalism.

Given this, suppose that $T_{A}$ emerges from $T_{B}$, but that $T_{A}$ is not syntactically complete. So there are sentences $\phi$ with signature ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ that some models of $T_{A}$ disagree on. In particular, for a fixed translation $\Gamma$ from $T_{A}$ into $T_{B}$, there may be two models $M$ and $N$ of $T_{B}$, where the associated models $\Gamma^{M}$ and $\Gamma^{N}$ disagree on $\phi$ (even though those two models are required to agree on all the sentences in $T_{A}$, along with all logical consequences of those sentences). Given such a $\phi$, the emergence of $T_{A}$ from $T_{B}$ is not faithful, and even if $\Gamma(\phi)$ is a logical consequence of $T_{B}$, it doesn’t follow that $\phi$ is satisfied in both $\Delta^{M}/E^{M}$ and $\Delta^{N}/E^{N}$. On the other hand, there might also be pairs of ${\mathcal{L}}_{B}$ structures $M^{\prime},N^{\prime}$ that both satisfy $T_{B}$ where the structures $\Delta^{M^{\prime}}/E^{M^{\prime}}$ and $\Delta^{N^{\prime}}/E^{N^{\prime}}$ do agree on that same $\phi$. So whether $\phi$ is taken to be true depends on what models of $T_{B}$ we are considering.

Now suppose that we have some measurable set $\mathscr{M}$ of models of $T_{B}$ and an associated probability measure, $Pr$. If all four models $M,N,M^{\prime},N^{\prime}$ are contained in $\mathscr{M}$, then there is an associated probability of whether the sentence $\phi$ in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ is true. This motivates the following definition.

**Definition 6.3**. (Stochastic Emergence) Suppose that a syntactically incomplete theory $T_{A}$ emerges from a consistent theory $T_{B}$ using a translation $\Gamma=(\Delta,E,\{\varphi_{s}\}_{s\in\Sigma_{A}})$. Suppose as well that $\mathscr{M}$ is a measurable set of models of $T_{B}$, and let $Pr_{\mathscr{M}}$ be a measure over $\mathscr{M}$. ^def-6-3

Write $Pr_{A}$ for the probability measure over whether the sentences in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ hold, induced by evaluating $Pr_{\mathscr{M}}$ over the structures $\{\Delta^{M}/E^{M}:M\in\mathscr{M}\}$ (which necessarily all satisfy $T_{A}$). We say that $(T_{A},Pr_{A})$ **stochastically emerges** from $(T_{B},Pr_{\mathscr{M}})$.

Probabilistic emergence and stochastic emergence propagate probability in two complementary ways. Probabilistic emergence propagates a probability measure over the elements of a domain, from a lower theory $T_{B}$ to an upper theory $T_{A}$, and thereby assigns a probability to the event that a property, some relation in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$, holds of a randomly chosen tuple of elements in the upper theory’s domain. Stochastic emergence instead propagates a measure over models of $T_{B}$, and thereby assigns a probability to the event that a law, some sentence of ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$, holds at all. In terms of our framework, the measure $Pr_{\mathscr{M}}$ in stochastic emergence is a probability measure over a set of physical systems, all governed by the scientific formalism $T_{B}$. It induces a probability measure over the possible logical consequences of the axioms defining the formalism $T_{A}$ that emerges from $T_{B}$.

A refined notion of stochastic emergence arises if we restrict how uncertain the truth of any of the sentences in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$ can be, under the measure $Pr_{\mathscr{M}}$:

**Definition 6.4**. (Confident Emergence) Suppose that $(T_{A},Pr_{A})$ stochastically emerges from $(T_{B},Pr_{\mathscr{M}})$. Suppose as well that for some $\epsilon\in[0,1/2)$, $Pr_{A}(\phi)\not\in[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]$ for all sentences $\phi$ that can be formulated in ${\mathcal{L}}_{A}$. Then we say that $(T_{A},Pr_{A})$ **confidently emerges** from $(T_{B},Pr_{\mathscr{M}})$ to within **precision** $\epsilon$. ^def-6-4

Confident emergence between theories may not be transitive, since errors may compound along chains of confident emergences. This provides a formal reason why long sequences of emergence of one formalism from another degrade even when each link is nearly exact.

### Multiple Emergence

It is not the case that (for example) biology emerges solely from chemistry. It also involves Newtonian mechanics, e.g., to describe the tensile strength of vertebrate bones. To capture this kind of phenomenon we need a way of having one formalism ${\mathcal{B}}$ emerge from a set of multiple other formalisms, $\{{\mathcal{A}}_{i}\}$. That would mean that some formulas in ${\mathcal{B}}$ can be interpreted in ${\mathcal{A}}_{1}$, while some other formulas in ${\mathcal{B}}$ can be interpreted in ${\mathcal{A}}_{2}$. In addition, often there are also coupling formulas, that involve both ${\mathcal{A}}_{1}$ and ${\mathcal{A}}_{2}$. As an illustration some of the formulas in biology involve just chemistry, while others involve just Newtonian mechanics. However, there are also formulas in biology that couple those formalisms, e.g., formulas coupling the Newtonian force on a particular bone to formulas governing the ratio of osteoclasts to osteoblasts inside that bone.

We formalize this as follows:

**Definition 6.5**. (Multiple emergence) Let $\{{\mathcal{A}}^{i}:i\in I\}$ be an indexed set of **basis** theories, with signatures $\{{\mathcal{L}}_{i}:i\in I\}$. Let $\rho$ be a **coupling** theory, with signature ${\mathcal{L}}_{\rho}$. Suppose we have an associated theory ${\mathcal{B}}$ where: ^def-6-5

1. ${\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{B}}={\mathcal{L}}_{\rho}\sqcup\coprod_{i\in I}{\mathcal{L}}_{i}$;
2. ${\mathcal{B}}$ is multi-sorted, in that the infinite set of variables in the language of ${\mathcal{B}}$ is partitioned, into a collection of sorts, one sort for $\rho$ and one for each theory $i\in I$. Write that set of variables of ${\mathcal{B}}$ in terms of the sorts as $X^{{\mathcal{L}}_{B}}=X^{\rho}\cup\bigcup_{i\in I}X^{i}$;
3. For all $i\in I$, every relation or function in ${\mathcal{L}}_{i}$ only involves the variables in the associated sort, $X^{i}$;
4. For all pairs of basis theories $i,j\neq i$, there are functions or relations in $\rho$ that involves both basis sorts $X^{i}$ and $X^{j}$.

Then we say that ${\mathcal{B}}$ is **multiple interpretable** in the set of theories $\{{\mathcal{A}}^{i}\}$ with **coupling theory** $\rho$. If some other theory ${\mathcal{C}}$ is emergent from such a ${\mathcal{B}}$, then we say that ${\mathcal{C}}$ is **multiple emergent** from the set of theories $\{{\mathcal{A}}^{i}\}$ with **coupling theory** $\rho$.

Note that due to the third requirement in [[#^def-6-5|Definition 6.5]], all functions in any one of the basis signatures are partial functions.

**Example 6.1**. Recall that Maxwell’s equations specify the electric and magnetic fields throughout a region of space based on the positions and velocities of all the particles in that region. In turn, the electromagnetic forces on any (possibly moving) charged particle are determined by the local electric and magnetic fields. ^ex-6-1

Accordingly, electrodynamics – the theory determining the dynamics of a Newtonian set of $N$ moving charged particles – is multiple emergent from Newton’s laws and Maxwell’s equations. Concretely, ${\mathcal{A}}^{1}$ is Newton’s laws for a set of $N$ particles, and ${\mathcal{A}}^{2}$ is Maxwell’s equations. The associated coupling theory $\rho$ provides the functions giving the Newtonian forces on the $N$ particles due to the electric and magnetic fields. It also provides the function (just the identity function, in this trivial case) setting the charges and velocities in Maxwell’s equations to the values given by the associated quantities in Newton’s laws. Finally, the auxiliary constraints give the initial conditions of the $N$ particles.

## Relation with what’s colloquially called “emergence”

In this section we show how our formalism captures some of the many phenomena that are informally called “emergence” in the literature.

### Coarse-graining as equivalence classes in a lower theory

Standard coarse-graining is a special case of the equivalence relation $E$ in Definition [[#^def-3-1|3.1]]. A partition of a state space $X$ into macroscopic bins $\{X_{\alpha}\}$ defines an equivalence relation, where $x\sim y$ iff $x$ and $y$ belong to the same bin. The macroscopic “state” is an equivalence class, and macroscopic observables are functions that are invariant under this equivalence, which is exactly the condition that model-theoretic interpretation requires of the translated symbols. This partition picture is a statement at the level of structures (Definition [[#^def-3-1|3.1]]), and a single microscopic state space is quotiented by $E$. The commutativity requirement introduced next is different in kind, and once it is demanded to hold uniformly across all models of the microscopic theory, it becomes a condition at the level of theories.

The canonical commutativity requirement considered in the literature on what “emergence” means in the case of coarse-graining provides a further constraint. Given a microscopic dynamics $f:X\to X$ and a coarse-graining map $\pi:X\to Y$, the emergent dynamics $g:Y\to Y$ satisfies commutativity when $\pi\circ f=g\circ\pi$. In model-theoretic terms, the micro-level structure $\mathcal{B}$ has the dynamics $f$ in its signature, and the macro-level structure $\mathcal{A}$ has $g$ in its signature. Commutativity says that the interpretation map $\Gamma$ (which includes $\pi$ as the encoding from $A$ into equivalence classes of $B$) preserves the dynamical sentence, and the sentence “$g$ maps state $\alpha$ to state $\beta$” in $T_{A}$ translates to a sentence provable in $T_{B}$. Commutativity is thus a consequence of interpretability when the theories include dynamical axioms.

But commutativity fails for many natural coarse-grainings. Not every partition of a chaotic state space yields a Markov process on the partition elements. In model-theoretic language, the failure means that the proposed $\Gamma$ does not yield an interpretation, and the translated dynamical sentence is not provable from $T_{B}$. When it fails, one must either change the partition (change $E$) or add auxiliary constraints $C$ restricting to initial conditions for which the commutativity approximately holds. The latter is stochastic emergence.

### Symmetry breaking

Symmetry breaking has two complementary readings in our framework. The collective modes of a large coupled system are an instance of multiple emergence, and the selection of one broken-symmetry ground state is an instance of stochastic emergence. Taking the collective modes first, recall that, as discussed briefly in[^cite-wk-2], a very large system of spins on a lattice can support propagation of phonons, i.e., waves in spin orientation that move through the lattice. To model this as a multiple emergent scenario, suppose we have a (thermodynamically) large number of basis theories ${\mathcal{A}}^{i}$, all of which are identical. (So they all contain the same set of sentences in the same language.) These theories will represent the individual spins in our system, with the sentences in each ${\mathcal{A}}^{i}$ providing those properties of spins that will be necessary for supporting the propagation of phonons.

Next, let the signature of all those theories each contain a special constant, which we call the “position”. Finally, suppose our coupling theory $\rho$ assigns a separate location on the lattice to the position constant of each of the individual theories, by fixing them to lie on an actual lattice in Euclidean space. The coupling theory also provides the [Hamiltonian function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ising_model) that couples each spin in the lattice to its neighbors, along with the associated dynamic laws governing how each spin’s state evolves as a function of the joint state of its neighbors. Concretely, if the basis theories involve quantum mechanics, and the spins are not entangled, then one of the sentences in $\rho$ will be Schrodinger’s equation, operating over the product of the wave functions of the individual spins. If instead we are using a semi-classical approximation of the system of spins, then the precise details of that approximation will provide the dynamics of the full system rather than Schrodinger’s equation..

Finally, let $T$ be a theory that is multiple emergent from those basis theories and that coupling theory, with auxiliary constraints that specify the initial state of the spin system. $T$ contains sentences that describe the propagation of phonons across large lattices. However, no set of sentences in the individual basis provides a description of such a process. So there are dynamic properties of the full, large-scale system that are completely absent from the individual, micro-scale subsystems. In particular, there are symmetry relations that are obeyed by individual spins but not by a thermodynamically large set of coupled spins. This is an example of Anderson had in mind when he wrote that “more is different”, and that “broken symmetry” arises as go from microscopic scale to macroscopic condensed matter systems.

The selection among degenerate ground states is the second reading. A crystal obeys quantum electrodynamics, but QED does not determine which crystal structure will form, and the ground state breaks the rotational symmetry of the Hamiltonian. In the language of phase transitions, the Hamiltonian is parameterized by a control parameter (temperature $T$, magnetic field $h$), and below a critical value of the control parameter the system selects one of several degenerate ground states characterized by a nonzero order parameter (magnetization, crystal orientation).

Model theory captures part of this. An incomplete theory $T_{B}$ (such as the theory of a Hamiltonian at a given temperature) has multiple models that disagree on sentences not entailed by $T_{B}$. In the ferromagnet, $T_{B}$ at zero field below $T_{c}$ does not entail the sentence “the magnetization is positive” or its negation, and both are satisfied by some model of $T_{B}$. Adding the auxiliary constraint $C=\{\text{“magnetization}>0\text{”}\}$ narrows the model class and makes the magnetized theory interpretable. The different choices of $C$ (positive versus negative magnetization) correspond to different broken-symmetry phases.

This is the paradigmatic case of stochastic emergence in the sense of Definition [[#^def-6-3|6.3]]. The two broken-symmetry phases are not merely two admissible models, and in the absence of an external field they carry a symmetric measure, each occurring with probability one half. Writing $\mathscr{M}$ for the models of $T_{B}$ below $T_{c}$ and $Pr_{\mathscr{M}}$ for that measure, the magnetized theory holds in a measure-one set of models once one conditions on either phase, while the symmetric $\tfrac{1}{2}$–$\tfrac{1}{2}$ split over the two phases is itself the emergent-level datum that no single uniform constraint can recover. This is why symmetry breaking is stochastic emergence rather than strict interpretability, in that the split among models of $T_{B}$ is real, but it is governed by a measure rather than eliminated by an axiom.

What model theory, even equipped with a measure, does not supply is the dynamics of selection. Which ground state is realized depends on the system’s history, on infinitesimal perturbations, and on boundary conditions. The framework classifies the possible outcomes and assigns them probabilities, but is silent on why a particular phase obtains on a given occasion. The control parameter $T$ and the order parameter $m$ are both expressible in $\mathcal{L}_{B}$ (they are definable functions on the domain), but the critical value $T_{c}$ at which the phase transition occurs is a property of the theory, and it is the value at which $T_{B}$ passes from completeness to incompleteness with respect to sentences about $m$. This locates phase transitions precisely in the model-theoretic architecture, as the points at which a parameterized family of theories loses completeness with respect to an order-parameter sentence.

## Computational complexity and the limits of interpretability

Model-theoretic interpretability of theories is an existence claim, asserting that there exists a special type of translation $\Gamma$ that maps $T_{A}$ into $T_{B}$, obeying the conditions listed in [[#^def-3-3|Definition 3.3]]. It says nothing about the computational cost of constructing or evaluating $\Gamma$.

When the domains are countable and the translation $\Gamma$ is (Turing) computable, the width and blow-up of the translation, defined in [[#Richness, expressivity, and parsimony|Section 5.2]], are measures of the compression that the emergent theory provides. We can define the usual computational complexity classes in terms of that blow-up. For example, a sentence $\omega$ in $\mathcal{L}_{A}$ of length $n$ may translate to a sentence $\Gamma(\omega)$ of length $n^{k}$ for some $k$ (polynomial) or $2^{n}$ (exponential). However, when dealing with finite theories, the blow-up $\widehat{\{\varphi_{s}\}}$ is finite, and so typically the number of symbols in $\Gamma(\omega)$ is no more than $\widehat{\{\varphi_{s}\}}$ times the length $\omega$. So in this case, the complexity is just linear.

A deeper question concerns the (Turing) decidability of the interpretation relation itself. Given two finitely axiomatized theories $T_{A}$ and $T_{B}$, can an algorithm determine whether $T_{A}\trianglelefteq T_{B}$? For broad classes of theories, the answer is no[^cite-wk-41]. Even when an interpretation exists, there may be no algorithm for discovering it. We cannot in general compute the lattice of interpretability degrees, and can only explore it piecemeal.

Sometimes an upper, “macroscopic” theory that involves dynamics emerge from a lower, “microscopic” that also involves dynamics. Moreover, sometimes there are properties of the macroscopic dynamics that can only be determined by simulating the full microscopic dynamics step by step. In our framework, this corresponds to an interpretation $\Gamma$ that exists but whose evaluation on a given model requires time proportional to the full simulation. The interpretation is logically valid but computationally useless, and knowing that $T_{A}\trianglelefteq T_{B}$ does not help you predict the behavior of a particular model of $T_{B}$ any faster than brute simulation.

This connects to a question raised by the programming analogy. A compiler maps a high-level program into machine code, and the map runs in polynomial time. But the execution of the resulting machine code, on a particular structure (a particular chip with particular inputs), may take exponential time. Knowing that $T_{A}\trianglelefteq T_{B}$ tells you only that a translation exists and is cheap to write down, and it says nothing about how expensive that translation is to evaluate on a given model. Emergence is the cheap, theory-level fact, and simulation is the expensive, model-level one. Model theory certifies the former and is silent on the latter.

The interested reader is directed to the literature on finite model theory, in which issues concerning the computational complexity of interpretations plays a central role[^cite-wk-23][^cite-wk-25].

## Model Theory in The Wild

The programming example of Section [[#From Languages and Machine Code to Hardware|4]] is misleading as an exemplar of scientific emergence, because the compiler was designed, and its interpretation map $\Gamma$, its equivalence relation $E$, and its auxiliary constraints $C$ were all specified in advance rather than discovered. Most scientific emergence is not like this. No one designed the mapping between neural activity and mental representation, or between individual behavior and collective dynamics. If those maps can be formalized as interpretations at all, they are products of evolution, development, or self-organization. We examine two such cases, brain and mind, and individuals and collectives, to see where the formalism marks the difference between evolution and engineering.

### Brain and mind

Consider the claim that a theory of mental states $T_{\text{mind}}$ emerges from a theory of neural activity $T_{\text{neural}}$. In the model-theoretic framework, this requires a translation $\Gamma$ that constructs the sorts, functions, and relations of $T_{\text{mind}}$ from formulas in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{neural}}$.

In the compiler case, the signatures of both $T_{H}$ and $T_{M}$ are fixed before the interpretation begins, along with the associated theories. We know what “integer addition” means in $\mathcal{L}_{H}$, i.e., we know the sentences that describe how the symbol for integer addition combines with the symbols for other concepts in the high level language. Similarly, we know what “word-level ADD” means in $\mathcal{L}_{M}$. The compiler translates the one into the other.

For brain and mind the associated signatures aren’t settled. In particular, though it’s not phrased this way, the signature of $T_{\text{mind}}$ (the non-logical symbols for belief, desire, intention, percept, affect) is a matter of ongoing dispute among cognitive scientists, philosophers, and psychologists. Different theoretical traditions assign different arities to these symbols, disagree about whether they are function symbols or relation symbols, and in some cases deny their existence altogether. The signature of $T_{\text{neural}}$ is comparatively better defined (firing rates, synaptic weights, receptor densities, network topology) but is itself a moving target as new measurement technologies (calcium imaging, Neuropixels, connectomics) reveal structure at finer resolutions.

When neither signature is fixed, the interpretation problem changes character. One is not translating a given vocabulary into another given vocabulary, but simultaneously discovering both vocabularies and the mapping between them. In engineered systems, signatures precede interpretation. In natural systems, the act of interpretation participates in the construction of the signatures. (Never mind the associated sentences.) This is why in previous work we have described Reality as an Oroborous, a snake that eats its own tail. In order to make foundational claims in most complex systems, one needs to borrow from a ”higher” or composite measurement, thereby shifting what is foundational to another levels of organization.

In the compiler, the equivalence relation $E$ in the lower theory is easily stated as two bit-patterns either encode the same integer or they do not. There is no ambiguity and no context-dependence.

Neural equivalence classes are very different. What counts as “the same mental state” across different patterns of neural activity is not a binary relation that can be read off the neural domain. The neuroscience of multi-voxel pattern analysis and neural decoding all involve imposing equivalence structures on high-dimensional activation spaces, but these structures are observer-dependent (they require a choice of similarity metric), context-dependent (the same neuron may participate in different equivalence classes depending on the task), and they are approximate (the boundaries of the classes are graded). In the model-theoretic formalism, $E$ must be a definable equivalence relation on $B^{k}$ for some $k$. If the “correct” equivalence relation is not definable in $\mathcal{L}_{\text{neural}}$ (because it depends on the organism’s history, or on environmental context that lies outside the neural domain) then the interpretation cannot be carried out within the neural theory alone. One needs an expanded lower theory that includes the environment, the developmental history, or both. The auxiliary constraints $C$ are doing much heavier work than in the engineered case, and it is not clear that they can be finitely axiomatized.

In the engineered case, the interpretation $\Gamma$ is an explicit, surveyable object. One can inspect the compiler’s source code, verify its correctness, trace its action on any particular input. For brain and mind, no such object exists. The “compiler” is the product of roughly 600 million years of nervous system evolution and roughly 20 years of individual neural development. It is not written in any inspectable language. Its existence is conjectured on the basis of correlational evidence (neural imaging, lesion studies, optogenetics) and theoretical argument (functionalism, computational theories of mind), but the actual map $\Gamma$, if it exists, has never been exhibited.

Model theory is indifferent to this gap. Interpretability is an existence claim, asserting that there exists a translation satisfying certain properties. Whether anyone has constructed the translation, or whether it is constructible in principle by a finite agent, is not a question the formalism addresses. The claim that $T_{\text{mind}}\trianglelefteq T_{\text{neural}}\cup C$ may be true yet permanently beyond our ability to verify, not because of computational cost (the worry of Section [[#Computational complexity and the limits of interpretability|8]]) but because the interpretation map is not the sort of thing that admits explicit construction. It was assembled by a historical process, not a logical one.

The extensions to model theory introduced above clarify much of what makes the brain-mind relation harder than the compiler case. The compiler case illustrates one kind of multiple realizability directly, and the same interpretation $\Gamma$, proved from $T_{M}$, constructs $T_{H}$ inside every chip. Different chips are different structures $\mathcal{M}\models T_{M}$, so the variability is across models of a fixed $\Gamma$. The brain case involves a different kind of variability. Different people may encode the same concept through quite different patterns of neural activity, so there is no single $\Gamma$ that holds across individuals, and there is instead a family $\{\Gamma_{i}\}$ of candidate interpretations, one per individual, with the variability across the interpretations themselves rather than across models of one interpretation. This is exactly where the parallel to the compiler breaks down, and it is the confound to avoid, since the object that is stable across individuals is not $\Gamma$ but the emergent theory $T_{\text{mind}}$ that each $\Gamma_{i}$ induces.

With the confound removed, the extensions slot in cleanly, and this is the constructive payoff. Individual variation in $\Gamma$ is captured by stochastic emergence, and placing a measure over the family $\{\Gamma_{i}\}$, equivalently over individuals (models of $T_{\text{neural}}$), makes the emergent mental laws hold with probabilities, and “the same concept, different neural codes” becomes a distribution over interpretations. The graded, similarity-based character of mental states, evident in the multi-voxel and decoding analyses above, is captured by approximate emergence, with the decoding metric supplying the metric $d^{A}$ on emergent states. And the dependence of the “correct” equivalence relation $E$ on environment and developmental history, rather than being a matter of auxiliary constraints that cannot be finitely axiomatized, is captured by multiple emergence, and $T_{\text{mind}}$ emerges jointly from $T_{\text{neural}}$ and a coupled environmental or developmental theory (Definition [[#^def-6-5|6.5]]), with the missing structure living in a second basis theory rather than in an unbounded axiom list.

What none of these extensions supplies is a constructed $\Gamma$. Model theory and its probabilistic extensions certify the existence and structure of the interpretation, and they never exhibit it by a finite agent. The claim that $T_{\text{mind}}$ emerges from $T_{\text{neural}}$ may therefore be true yet permanently beyond our ability to verify, because the interpretation was assembled by a historical process rather than a logical one.

### Individuals and collectives

Consider now the claim that a theory of collective behavior $T_{\text{coll}}$ emerges from a theory of individual behavior $T_{\text{ind}}$.

In the compiler case, the “parts” of the machine (registers, ALU, memory cells) interact according to a fixed architecture specified by the hardware design. The interaction structure is part of the signature of $T_{M}$, and there are function symbols for reading from and writing to specified addresses, with the connectivity hard-wired.

In a collective (a colony of ants, a flock of birds, a market of traders, a population of neurons) the interaction structure is itself a variable. Who interacts with whom, when, and through what channel is not fixed by the theory of individual behavior, and it is an emergent property of the dynamics. The network of interactions is not in $\Sigma_{\text{ind}}$, and it arises from the interplay of individual strategies, spatial proximity, signalling, and environmental constraints. In model-theoretic terms, the interaction topology belongs not to $T_{\text{ind}}$ but to the auxiliary constraints $C$ or perhaps to an expanded signature.

This creates a bootstrapping problem. To interpret $T_{\text{coll}}$ in $T_{\text{ind}}\cup C$, we need $C$ to specify the interaction structure. But the interaction structure is itself a collective-level property, part of what $T_{\text{coll}}$ describes. The auxiliary constraints that enable emergence are drawn from the vocabulary of the emergent theory, not just from the vocabulary of the lower theory. This circularity has no obvious counterpart in the engineered case, where the hardware architecture is given independently of the software that runs on it.

What counts as “the same collective state” across different configurations of individuals? In a bird flock, two configurations that differ in the positions of individual birds but share the same velocity field, density profile, and polarization may be treated as equivalent. In a market, two microeconomic states that produce the same price, volume, and volatility may be regarded as the same macroeconomic state. These equivalence relations are not arbitrary, and they are selected by the collective dynamics itself. The macroscopic observables that define the equivalence classes are precisely those that are approximately preserved under the microscopic dynamics, and these are the slowly varying modes, the conserved quantities, and the order parameters.

In model-theoretic language, the equivalence relation $E$ is not given in advance (as in the compiler) or discovered by correlation (as in the brain-mind case), and it is selected by the dynamics from among the possible partitions of the microstates space. The correct $E$ is the one for which the interpretation $\Gamma$ yields a well-defined emergent dynamics satisfying commutativity (Section [[#Coarse-graining as equivalence classes in a lower theory|7.1]]). This means that the equivalence relation, the translation map, and the emergent theory are jointly determined, and they are not independent components to be specified one at a time, as they are in the engineered case. The interpretability problem in collective systems is a fixed-point problem, and one must find $E$, $\Gamma$, and $T_{\text{coll}}$ simultaneously such that the interpretation holds.

In the compiler case, $T_{M}$ is fixed, and the instruction set architecture does not change depending on what program is being compiled. In collective systems, the effective lower theory can shift as the collective organizes. Individual ants in a colony modify their behavioral repertoire in response to colony-level states (task allocation, alarm signals, food availability). Individual neurons modify their synaptic weights in response to network-level activity. The lower theory $T_{\text{ind}}$ is not a fixed set of axioms, and it is itself modulated by the emergent level. In model-theoretic terms, the axioms of $T_{\text{ind}}$ are functions of the model of $T_{\text{coll}}$ in which the individual is embedded.

## A Concluding Typology of Emergence

Recapping, in our framework, we identify the mathematically stated laws of a particular scientific formalism with a particular first order theory. A canonical example is Newton’s laws, by themselves. Note that this formalism does not specify any forces, masses, phase space positions, etc.

We then identify any particular physical system that obeys such a set of laws with a structure that satisfies that theory. That structure is where the forces, masses, and phase space positions are specified. A system of gravitating point masses in vacuum evolving according to Newton’s laws is an example.

If an upper formalism $T_{A}$ is interpretable in a lower formalism $T_{B}$ but not vice-versa, then we identify $T_{A}$ as emerging from $T_{B}$. More generally though, we also view $T_{A}$ as emerging from $T_{B}$ if $T_{A}$ is not interpretable in $T_{B}$, but it is interpretable in $T_{B}$ together with a finite set of auxiliary constraints, sentences of ${\mathcal{L}}_{B}$ that we add to $T_{B}$. This is the case for example when we view statistical mechanics of ideal gases as emerging from Newton’s laws, and we need to add special auxiliary constraints concerning the probability measure over the possible joint phase space positions of the particles in the gas in order to get statistical mechanics.

In addition, we use the term scientific discipline to refer to a set of formalisms that are mutually interpretable. Classical dynamics is an example, which comprises the formalisms of Newton’s laws, Lagrangian dynamics, Hamilton’s equations, etc. Since mutual interpretability is transitive, all formalisms in a given discipline emerge from one another.

The several phenomena that go under the name “emergence” can now be organized through a model-theoretic typology. A typology does not resolve the ambiguities of emergence, but it makes explicit the role of auxiliary constraints and of a measure over the models of the lower theory, and it shows that otherwise disparate accounts are manifestations of the same logical relation.

Strict interpretability ($T_{A}\trianglelefteq T_{B}$, no auxiliary constraints) covers cases where the emergent theory is fully constructible from the lower theory. The compiler example belongs here, as does the interpretation of the rationals inside the integers.

Emergence ($T_{A}\trianglelefteq T_{B}\cup C$, with auxiliary constraints) covers the typical case in physics, and includes thermodynamics from statistical mechanics, hydrodynamics from molecular dynamics, and effective field theories from more fundamental ones. The constraints $C$ encode typicality, boundary conditions, or scale separation. These all add additional parameters to theories leading to significant departures from parsimony.

Stochastic emergence covers cases where the emergent regularity holds with high probability under a natural measure. The ideal gas law holds for typical configurations in the microcanonical ensemble, and atypical configurations exist but have measure zero in the thermodynamic limit. Symmetry breaking is the other paradigm case, and below the critical temperature the broken-symmetry phases carry a measure, with the emergent theory holding in a measure-one set of models once a phase is fixed. This is different from the simpler idea of a probabilistic emergence function where the laws are fixed but the outcomes are distributions.

Multiple emergence covers the special sciences, which emerge from conjunctions of lower theories (biology from chemistry, mechanics, and information theory jointly) rather than from any single base. The relationship between biology and physics is of this kind. Organisms satisfy biological laws, but not all physical systems are organisms, and no finite axiom set picks out the biological ones, so biology emerges from physics jointly with organizational and selective constraints rather than from physics alone. This is also described as exaptation in the case where constraints can be apportioned to either a system (e.g. traits in an organisms) and to the environment (e.g. phases of matter). Hence a lung (providing a physiological interface for gas exchange) is an exaptation from a swim bladder (conferring neutral buoyancy in a fluid). We might also say that the mathematical abilities of current Large Language Models are exaptations from their original linguistic competence as natural language models. The change in environment or context in the case of an LLM is a change in syntax.

Expressive dependence and foundational dependence are not emergence at all, but structural relations between theories (expansion, inclusion, identity) that model theory also formalizes.

Realizational dependence is emergence applied to the mind-body problem, where the question of whether the cognitive sorts are definable in neural vocabulary remains open.

Epistemological dependence is a constraint on the admissible class of theories, not an interpretability relation between theories, and may require resources beyond first-order model theory to formalize.
