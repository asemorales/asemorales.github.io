Over the years, my view of automation has evolved to best be described by the following: Automate away everything that distracts you from being human.

The first section of this statement is hardly controversial at all, for many thinkers and builders have already suggested that the end goal of automation is to free up time for human flourishing. It is the exact definition of the last two words that incite controversy: "being human". The rest of the statement hinges on how this is defined[^1]. Once it is defined what constitutes a good human life, it then follows that the maximum bound of using automation is that which does not impede human well-being.

I do not go into the definition of what constitutes a good human life, which has already been debated in much greater depth by many philosophers. Rather, I try to describe here what the rest of the statement might imply from that definition.

## How may we define bounds on technological acceleration?

It seems there are two main constraints to this. First, given that the hard constraint is that technological use must not impede human well-being, it follows then that technological acceleration should also proceed at the rate that meets the same condition.

Second, technological acceleration is also a form of moving into the [[glossary#adjacent possible (noun)|adjacent possible]] (i.e. new technologies expand the set of immediately possible future technologies). Hence, the laws proposed by Kauffman to the adjacent possible also applies to technological acceleration:

> [!quote] Stuart Kauffman, [Investigations](https://archive.org/details/investigations00kauf)
> As an average trend, biospheres and the universe create novelty and diversity as fast as they can manage to do so without destroying the accumulated propagating organization that is the basis and nexus from which further novelty is discovered and incorporated into the propagating organization.

In other words, technological acceleration proceeds at the fastest[^3] possible rate that:
1. does not impede human well-being, whether directly or indirectly (i.e. through resource depletion); and
2. does not impede further advancements in society, technology, etc.

## How may we define bounds on technological adoption?

I don't have a clear answer here. I don't have enough background in social sciences to argue this in-depth, nor know what it may yet look like in practice. However, my general sentiment is this:

It seems to me a failure of industry-government-academe collaboration that large-scale adoptions of automation cause such devastating displacement. In principle, it should be possible to create social programs, collaborations, and safeguards that avoid significant [technological unemployment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_unemployment). Namely, any technological adoption that causes societal disruption should be accompanied by social programs with the aim of:
1. controlling the rate of technological adoption such that it prioritizes societal readiness over technological capability[^2]; and
2. aiding people through the transition

While the idea of social programs to ease people through technological adoption alone might seem far-fetched, it seems plausible that it would arise out of a necessary reaction to a global recession and loss of entry-level jobs. That is, public demand and complaints could plausibly force governments into developing these social programs. It may even be necessary in countries where a large share of the nation's economy is displaced by technological adoption, such as low- and middle- income countries whose economy is largely dependent on production or services exported to higher-income countries that have the capital to produce their own systems to automate away labor that was previously outsourced.

## How may we react to the automation of intellectual labor?

>[!quote]Alex Imas, *[What will be scarce?](https://aleximas.substack.com/p/what-will-be-scarce?utm_campaign=post-expanded-share&utm_medium=web)*
>When AI automates commodity production, prices in that sector fall. That raises real income. If the goods and services people want more of as they get richer lie disproportionately in the relational sector, demand shifts in that direction. Baumol’s cost disease then amplifies the result: if the relational sector remains harder to automate, it becomes relatively more expensive and absorbs a growing share of total expenditure.

 My speculation is that the history of automation proceeds from manual labor (production) -> manual labor (quality assurance) -> intellectual labor (production)[^4] -> intellectual labor (verification) -> labor that requires [embodied intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embodied_agent) and/or solving [Moravec’s paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravec's_paradox). If so, what remains is coordination and connection. Both of these require understanding of human behavior, which is understood through human experience, and thus is uniquely difficult to displace[^5]. That is, the relational sectors of childcare, healthcare, education, leadership, therapy, and so on.

    Thus for the first time since his creation man will be faced with his real, his permanent problem—how to use his freedom from pressing economic cares, how to occupy the leisure, which science and compound interest will have won for him, to live wisely and agreeably and well.

[^4]: > [!quote] Norbert Wiener, *[The Human Use of Human Beings](https://thepastpresented.com/?p=1639)*
    > [T]he machine plays no favorites between manual labor and white-collar labor. *Thus the possible fields into which the new industrial revolution is likely to penetrate are very extensive*, and include all labor performing judgments of a low level, in much the same way as the displaced labor of the earlier industrial revolution included every aspect of human power.

[^5]: As an example of what this might look like, we can look to the concept of *suki* in the Philippines. In Tagalog, *suki* refers to a seller that a customer usually purchases from out of loyalty and/or personal ties, moreso than due to the quality of the seller's product/s. This is especially common in wet markets (*palengke*), where multiple vendors sell the same products for similar prices and with similar quality. With little to no distinction between the actual products, buyers then purchase based on their relationship with the seller as a differentiator. We are very likely to see a similar trend occur as technological adoption causes more products and services to converge towards similar prices and quality, for which customer relations is the main differentiator.
