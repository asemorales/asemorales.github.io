Adaptability as a component, and sometimes a definition, of intelligence is a relatively well-developed concept across cognitive literature. The rest of this page looks at some examples in cognitive research and applications in evaluation of AI systems. There is far more literature beyond this, but seeing as this is rather well-established, the few below suffice to give an overview.

## In cognitive research

[Fluid intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_and_crystallized_intelligence) is, by definition, quite close to adaptability. As early as the 1920s, expert surveys repeatedly define intelligence as adaptability[^cite-thorndike-ims]. Piaget argues that intelligence stems from biological adaptation. Namely, he defines two mechanisms: assimilation and accommodation, where assimilation is pattern recognition into existing mental models of patterns and structures, and accommodation is changing existing mental models when current models do not fit the data[^cite-piaget-tpi].

Godfrey-Smith elaborates on this further, and posits that minds exist for organisms to adapt to complex environments[^cite-gs-cfmn]. [Rational analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_analysis) closely begins with the assumption that cognition is adapted to its environment (in contrast to traditional cognitive science), and the methods of rational analysis are founded on this assumption.

## In evaluation of AI systems

AI research has similarly been applying adaptability as a measure of intelligence. The ARC benchmark evaluates intelligence in AI systems based on he efficiency of skill acquisition (i.e. excluding existing skills)[^cite-chollet-arc], and other work compares the intelligence of biological to artificial intelligence on the basis of adaptability[^cite-bennett-absmitai].
